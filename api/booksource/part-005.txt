Jasper felt the words settle uncomfortably.

"Influence how?"

"The surviving records disagree. Some are later accounts. Some are partial translations from older Selari documents that were already being studied at the time."

Ava leaned forward. "Did the student cause the sea to move?" Professor Elowen paused.

"The student believed they could."

Jasper noticed the difference at once.

"Could they?"

"That," Elowen said, "is exactly the question people answered too confidently."

The lesson bell rang before anyone could ask more. Ava added the sentence to her notebook that afternoon. The student believed they could. Jasper read it several times. His dream had never felt as though he were controlling anything. The presence had answered, yes, but an answer was not obedience.

The library search also introduced them to the limits of public records. Ava discovered that Tidefall did not have one single perfect archive. Some materials had been digitised. Others existed only as paper, translated copies or references in books written decades later. Dates disagreed. Names changed spelling. Old Selari terms had several competing English translations. Soren seemed to enjoy this rather than finding it frustrating.

"It means you have to compare," he said.

Harper stared at him. "You say that as if it is good news."

"It is."

Ava nodded. "It is." Harper looked at Jasper. "We've lost her." The Deepwater gaps became more suspicious only after they understood how ordinary gaps looked. A missing cafeteria menu from twenty years ago meant nothing. A major safety revision referenced in three later policies but described nowhere in accessible detail meant something, though not necessarily conspiracy. When Professor Elowen explained that privacy rules covered the people involved, Ava wrote a note in the margin: MISSING ≠ HIDDEN FOR BAD REASON. Jasper liked that. It prevented the mystery from becoming an excuse to distrust everybody.

At the same time, the Selari fragment from the lower door gave them a second line of investigation that belonged to history rather than private lives. The old phrase about waiting where water passed appeared in a catalogue of early coastal warnings. Several similar inscriptions existed around ancient Tidefall structures. Whatever Deepwater had been, people had been cautious around unusual water long before it happened. He found himself thinking about that distinction long after the room lights went out.

CHAPTER TEN
THE THING HARPER COULD NOT DO SLOWLY

Harper Vale disliked doing anything slowly if a faster version existed. She walked quickly, ate quickly, answered questions before people finished asking them and opened presents with the efficiency of someone trying to defeat wrapping paper before it could regroup. Her magic had developed the same habit. By the middle of first term, this had become a problem. Professor Ward placed a ceramic cup of water in front of each student during Making and Mending. A temperature strip lay beside it.

"Warm the water by five degrees."

Harper raised her hand. "Can we do ten?"

"No."

"Why?"

"Because the exercise says five."

"That feels arbitrary."

"It is extremely deliberate."

Jasper's first attempt produced no measurable change. His second managed one degree, and his third reached four. Ava achieved exactly five on her second try and allowed herself half a second of satisfaction before pretending she had expected it. Harper's cup steamed. Ward was beside her immediately. "Stop." Harper lowered her hands. The strip settled seventeen degrees above the starting temperature. She brightened. "That's quite good."

"It is twelve degrees beyond the instruction."

"But I controlled it."

Ward looked at the steam. "Did you?" The next attempt reached fourteen. Then eleven. Then nine. Harper became more frustrated with every improvement because none of them was five, and the frustration made the heat gather faster. Jasper could see it in her shoulders before the cup even changed.

"Stop," Ward said.

"I nearly had it."

"Stop first. Be annoyed second."

Harper folded her arms. Ward sat on the edge of the demonstration table. "What is the exercise?"

"Warm the water five degrees."

"That's the target. What are you practising?"

Harper frowned. Ward tapped the temperature strip. "Stopping at the amount you chose. Power you cannot stop is not extra power. It is missing control." Nobody laughed. Ward had not said it harshly, which made Harper look even less comfortable. She tried again. Six degrees. Harper groaned.

"Good," Ward said.

"It was wrong."

"By one. Yesterday you were wrong by twelve."

Harper stared at the cup. Jasper understood something about her then. Harper enjoyed appearing fearless, but she hated being visibly bad at things. Those were related problems, not identical ones. After class she kicked a pebble across Founders' Courtyard.

"Stupid water."

Jasper looked at her. "The water didn't do anything."

"Exactly."

Ava walked on Harper's other side. "You improved."

"Don't."

"I am attempting support."

"You're doing it badly."

Ava considered this. "Would you prefer dishonesty?" Harper sighed. They reached the western wall, where the sea below was rough beneath the afternoon wind. Harper rested her arms on the safe inner ledge and watched the waves.

"Do you think I would've been good at whatever the Deepwater student tried?"

Jasper felt a chill that had nothing to do with weather. "What?"

"Professor Elowen said they thought they could influence the sea. If it was about power..."

Ava shook her head. "We have no evidence that it was."

"I know. I'm asking."

Jasper remembered Ward's sentence.

"Maybe that's why it went wrong."

Harper looked at him.

"Power you can't stop," Jasper said.

She turned back to the ocean. "Maybe." That evening, Harper practised with a mug in Room Fourteen. Nobody had assigned homework. She borrowed Ava's temperature strip, sat at the desk and tried again. Eight degrees. Seven. Six. Then five. Harper stared at the result. Ava deliberately looked away.

"Don't say anything," Harper warned.

"I wasn't going to."

Jasper grinned.

"You either."

He raised both hands. Harper tried once more and hit five again. She leaned back. "Easy." Ava made a sound that might have been a laugh. Harper threw a sock at her.

For several minutes the room became a small war. Bag Bed suffered most of the casualties and ended the evening with three socks, one pillow and Jasper's Ancient Selari workbook on top of its existing collection. Later, after lights-out, Harper spoke from the dark.

"Jasper?"

"Yeah?"

"If that sea thing happens again, don't try to make it do anything."

Jasper opened his eyes. "I wasn't going to."

"Good."

There was a pause.

"Why?"

Harper shifted under her blanket. "Because I don't like things you can't stop." Jasper looked toward the faint rectangle of the western window.

"Okay."

The ocean moved normally below the cliff. He wished he could promise it would keep doing that. Harper's control problem began affecting things outside class in quieter ways. She shut drawers too hard when annoyed. Her bedside lamp brightened during arguments. Once, while trying to pull a book from under Bag Bed, she accidentally dragged two other objects with it and sent the west-stair pebble rolling under Jasper's bed.

"Your magic is dramatic," Jasper said.

"My magic has personality."

Ava retrieved the pebble. "Your magic has poor impulse control." Harper threw a pillow at her without magic, which Ava called an improvement. Professor Ward began giving Harper stopping exercises rather than stronger ones. She practised bringing moving objects to rest at exact marks and lowering shields smoothly instead of dropping them the instant a threat ended. Harper hated the work because nobody watching would describe it as impressive. That was partly why it mattered.

Jasper saw the first real change during a corridor accident. A younger visitor on Parent Weekend dropped a glass bottle. Harper reacted before anyone else, catching it with a burst of force half a metre above the floor. The bottle stopped. For an instant Jasper expected it to shoot upward or slam into the wall. Instead Harper held it there, breathing carefully, then lowered it into her hand. The child's mother thanked her. Harper looked pleased for the rest of the afternoon but did not mention the temperature exercise once.

Harper could not do anything slowly if there was a faster option. This included walking. Eating. Opening presents. Answering questions she had only half heard. And magic. By the middle of first term, this had become a problem. Professor Ward demonstrated the exercise with a cup of water. Control, Jasper realised, was beginning to become useful before it became visible. He knew enough now not to try.

CHAPTER ELEVEN
THE LOWER DOOR

The trouble with rules, Jasper discovered, was that understanding them did not always make them less interesting. The lower-dock restriction had been easy to ignore when it was only a sign. After Deepwater, it became a sign connected to an old accident, unusual marine magic and a student who had believed they could control something they did not understand. This did not make Harper less curious. It made Ava more curious, which was considerably more dangerous because Ava knew how to use catalogues.

"We are not going through it," Ava said.

The three of them were standing in the corridor beside the lower-dock access door after lunch. Harper claimed they had been passing nearby. Jasper did not point out that the dining hall was two levels up and almost the length of the Academy away.

"I didn't say we were going through," Harper said. "We're looking."

The door was dark wood reinforced with metal bands. A silver ward plate had recently been fitted beside it, and beneath the plate a smaller brass notice listed supervised hours for younger students. Ava crouched to inspect the frame. "This part is newer."

"You can tell from wood?" Jasper asked.

"The hinge screws are different."

Harper looked at him. "This is why we keep her."

"I can hear you."

"Good."

Jasper's attention drifted to the stone around the doorway. The blocks nearest the frame were pale and recently cut, but one section farther back carried shallow marks almost erased by time. He stepped closer.

"Ava."

She looked up.

"Is that writing?"

The three leaned toward the stone without crossing the red boundary line. Under several layers of repair marks were four angular characters. Harper squinted. "Selari?" Ava took out her notebook. "Definitely older script." Jasper recognised one character from class and felt absurdly pleased about it.

"That one is water. I think."

"Sava," Ava said.

"Right."

"Maybe," Harper added helpfully.

Ava ignored her. "The second character looks like the old form Professor Ilyan showed us for boundary or threshold."

"So it says water door?" Harper asked.

"No. That is not how translation works."

"It would be a good name."

They were still arguing when the ward plate flashed once. The lock clicked. All three froze. The door opened two centimetres. Cold, salt-heavy air breathed through the gap. Nobody touched it. Harper's face changed from delighted to cautious so quickly Jasper almost missed it.

"Did you do that?" she whispered.

Ava shook her head. Jasper felt the pressure behind his ribs. Faint. Deep. Familiar. He took one step backward. The door remained slightly open. Professor Ward's voice came from behind them. "That is an impressive collection of guilty expressions." They turned. Ward walked toward the door, took in the ward light and the gap, and became serious at once.

"None of you touched the plate?"

"No," Ava said.

"Crossed the line?"

"No."

Ward checked the ward with a small brass token. The plate flashed red, then blue.

"Back to the main corridor."

Harper opened her mouth.

"Now."

That ended discussion. The door was closed and re-warded before dinner. By evening, a temporary notice had appeared instructing everyone to use the eastern stair for supervised harbour access until maintenance finished inspecting the lock. Professor Ilyan found them in Ancient Selari the next morning and wrote the four characters from the door on the board.

"I understand some of you discovered these yesterday."

Harper looked toward Jasper. "We're famous."

"You're loud," Ava corrected.

Ilyan tapped the inscription. "The best current translation is not water door." Harper looked wounded.

"It is closer to: where the water passes, wait."

Jasper stared at the words.

"A warning?"

"Possibly. Or an instruction. The grammar predates several standard forms. Context matters."

Ava wrote every word. After class, Jasper copied the inscription into his own notes. For the first time, Ancient Selari did not feel like a school subject somebody had added because old languages were respectable. It had been cut into Tidefall's stone before the present door existed. Whatever people here had been dealing with, it had started long before him.

The problem with rules was that some of them became more interesting after you understood why they existed. The lower-dock rule was one of those. Before Deepwater, it had been a sign. YEARS ONE TO THREE: STAFF SUPERVISION REQUIRED.

After Deepwater, it was a sign connected to a twenty-three-year-old event involving unusual sea magic, a student making a dangerous choice and someone getting hurt. This did not make Harper less interested. It made Ava more interested, which was much worse.

The door was ordinary dark wood reinforced with metal bands. Beside it, a small plaque displayed access hours and rules. Year Four and above could use the lower docks during daylight open periods after completing marine safety training. Years One to Three required staff supervision at all times. Temporary red closure notices overrode everything. There was no ambiguity. Ava read every line anyway.

Ava shut the notebook. That should have been the end. Then the door clicked. All three stared. The handle moved down by itself. Not fast. Slowly. The latch released. The door opened two centimetres. Cold salt air moved through the gap. Harper's eyes widened. Ava whispered, "We didn't touch it." Jasper's chest became heavy. Very heavy. The gap widened another centimetre. From below came the distant crash of water inside the cliff harbour. Harper stepped toward it. Jasper grabbed her sleeve.

That evening Thorne came to Room Fourteen. Headmasters did not normally come to first-year bedrooms. Harper immediately sat up straighter. Ava moved three papers off Bag Bed. Jasper stood beside his bed. Thorne remained near the door. That thought should have made him feel less responsible. Instead, it made the Academy seem much older beneath his feet.

CHAPTER TWELVE
RAIN ON THE WESTERN WINDOWS

Summer ended slowly. The mornings cooled first. Then the afternoon wind sharpened. By the beginning of autumn, Room Fourteen's western window spent more time shut than open, and Bag Bed had acquired two extra blankets because Harper said they might need them in an emergency. Ava asked what kind of emergency required unused blankets but not the actual beds. Harper said a cold one. Jasper loved the rain. Mostly.

School changed with the weather. Ocean Studies moved indoors on rough days. The courtyard handball games became more competitive because running kept everyone warm. Students discovered which corridors leaked during heavy rain and arranged buckets before teachers could. At home, winter storms had always meant staying inside near windows. At Tidefall, weather became part of the building. Wind hummed through old stone. Rain rattled against sea-facing glass. Waves struck the cliff hard enough that Jasper sometimes felt them through the floor. The presence did not return strongly. That made it easier to live. The lower door was replaced. A new silver ward plate appeared beside it. Jasper walked past without stopping. The trio's first proper argument happened because of a handball. Harper said it was not an argument. Ava said volume did not determine category.

Jasper said they were both annoying. That stopped Harper.

That made them unite against him, which solved it. The actual problem began after dinner when Harper invited two other first-years to Room Fourteen without asking. Mia brought a deck of cards. Soren brought a book because Soren always brought a book. Ava had planned to use the desk for a Foundations assignment. Harper had not known this. Ava believed she should have asked. Harper believed rooms were for people. Ava believed rooms were also for work. Harper believed work was what study rooms were for. Ava believed friendship did not mean everyone got to decide what happened in shared space without checking. Mia said, "We can go." Harper immediately said, "No."

Then looked at Ava.

"Sorry. Should I have asked?"

Ava blinked. The argument vanished so quickly it almost looked disappointed.

"Yes."

"Okay. Sorry."

Ava moved her books to one end of the desk.

"You can stay."

Mia sat down. Soren had never moved. Jasper learned another thing about his friends. Harper could apologise fast when she understood the problem. Ava could let go fast when she believed the apology. He wondered what his own annoying thing was. He did not ask. He was not ready. They played cards until Mae told them quiet hour had begun. After Mia and Soren left, rain began against the western windows. Harper switched off the main lamp.

"Storm watching."

Ava said, "We have school tomorrow."

"Storm watching quietly."

They sat on Bag Bed together because it had the best angle toward the western window. Dark cloud covered the sea. Distant lightning flashed low over the horizon. No thunder reached them yet. Jasper watched waves appear in white lines and vanish.

"Do you miss home?" Ava asked suddenly.

Harper answered first.

"
