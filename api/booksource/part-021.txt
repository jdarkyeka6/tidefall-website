Jasper understood. They had spent so much time near the Sea mystery that it had begun to bend memory around itself. But first year had also been burnt toast in the dining hall. Theo throwing parchment balls. Mia teaching Harper a clapping game from home. Ava discovering the library cataloguing system and becoming offended by three shelf labels. Jasper writing letters on Sunday afternoons. Rain rattling the western windows. Learning how not to set a practice cloth on fire. The first time their shield held. The first time their shelter fell down. The first time Jasper got homesick enough that he nearly cried over a jar of his mother's jam. A year was not one mystery.

They titled their section THINGS YOU ARE ALLOWED NOT TO KNOW YET. Under it they wrote: You are allowed to get lost. You are allowed to miss home. You are allowed to ask what a word means. You are allowed to be bad at a spell before you are good at it. You are allowed to change your mind about people. You are allowed to tell a teacher when something feels wrong. You are allowed to say you are scared and still be brave. You are allowed not to turn every strange thing into a dare. Harper objected to the last one.

Jasper added another. You are allowed to have a problem you cannot solve by yourself. Nobody joked about that one. When the class shared drafts two days later, Room Twelve had written a brilliant guide to laundry magic. Room Nine had made a map of the best places to sit when you wanted company and the best places to sit when you did not. Theo's group had written an entire page titled FOOD MISTAKES. Elowen read it twice.

The Chronicle became larger than the original assignment. Students added sketches. Older students contributed annotations. The infirmary added a page explaining when to stop pretending a sprain was fine. The kitchens added a guide to requesting food for allergies, homesickness and religious or family traditions without feeling awkward. Crowe added a page titled WHEN A RULE DOESN'T MAKE SENSE, ASK WHY. Harper accused her of impersonating Ava. Then Ava found the safety-history page. It sat between "Cliff Boundaries" and "Storm Signals."

Ava read it standing beside the Chronicle table in the library. The new paragraph was short. More than two decades earlier, a student with an unusual sensitivity to the Sea repeatedly tested that connection near the lower harbour. During one incident, an unexpected surge injured another student. Both recovered. Tidefall subsequently revised lower-harbour access, supervision and incident-recording procedures. The event remains part of staff safety training. No pretending it had been only weather. No pretending nobody had been hurt. Just the next class. They ran anyway.

CHAPTER SIXTY-EIGHT
FOUR OPEN DOORS

Order Open Week began with Brannor trying to teach first-years how to carry a bucket without spilling it. Harper looked offended by the lack of swords.

"This is courage?"

The Brannor sixth-year supervising the activity was called Priya Nand, and she had the calm expression of someone who had heard this question before.

"No. This is carrying water."

Harper stared. Priya continued.

"The courage lesson is later."

"When?"

"If I tell you, you will perform for it."

Ava smiled. Harper did not. Open Week was Tidefall's answer to first-year Order panic. Instead of asking seven-year-olds or older students to explain the Orders through slogans, each Order opened part of its normal life to first-years for an afternoon. No recruitment speeches. No promises that one Order was better. No tests. Just participation. The first afternoon belonged to Brannor. Jasper had expected obstacle courses. There was one. It came after two hours of carrying water, repairing storm boxes, checking emergency lanterns and learning how to tie a rescue line so it could be released under tension. Harper loved the knots more than she admitted.

"Again," she told Priya after the third attempt.

"You got it right."

"I can get it faster."

"Why?"

Harper opened her mouth. Closed it. Priya waited.

"Because if someone needs it, faster matters."

"Good answer. Now do it slowly."

Harper's face became tragic. Jasper laughed. Brannor's common spaces surprised him. They were not filled with trophies. There were some. Old shields hung above one fireplace. A cracked rescue bell sat in a glass case. But most of the walls showed groups. Students rebuilding a jetty. Students carrying supplies after floods. Students cooking in enormous pots during a winter outage. One photo showed Brannor students sitting in a circle around another student who looked miserable. The caption read: COURAGE IS SOMETIMES STAYING. Jasper stopped at that. Priya noticed.

"Everyone expects Brannor to be the running-toward-fire Order."

Harper, from behind them, said, "It isn't?"

"Sometimes there is a fire."

"Excellent."

"Sometimes courage is admitting you caused it."

Harper looked less excited. Priya smiled.

"Sometimes it's apologising. Sometimes it's asking for help. Sometimes it's being the first person to say a plan is stupid when everyone else is cheering."

Ava said, "Harper may struggle with that last one."

"I can say plans are stupid."

"Your own plans."

"Why would I make a stupid plan?"

Priya looked between them.

"You three are going to make somebody's mentoring year very interesting."

The obstacle course arrived last. It was not about speed. Each group had to move four crates through a yard while keeping one person on a marked platform at all times. The obvious solution was to carry crates quickly. The better solution was to move the platform marker too. Harper figured that out only after trying to sprint the first crate across and getting called back. Ava figured it out immediately. Jasper was the one who noticed the rule said one person had to remain on "a marked platform," not "the starting platform." They won by thirty seconds. Harper took full credit. The next day was Velmor. Velmor's wing had higher windows than Brannor's and too many staircases.

Jasper understood within five minutes why older Velmor students seemed permanently aware of weather. Their afternoon started with no instructions at all. A teacher handed each group a compass, a sealed envelope and a map with three sections missing.

"Find the observatory."

Harper grinned. Ava said, "This is irresponsible." The teacher smiled.

"There are staff at every junction."

"Then it is controlled irresponsibility."

"That's one interpretation."

They spent an hour moving through upper passages Jasper had never seen. Velmor did not reward the group that arrived first. It rewarded groups for how many different routes they documented, what they noticed and whether they helped anyone else who was lost. Jasper liked that. Harper hated that she could not win by running. Ava loved the map until she discovered one blank section was intentionally impossible to complete because a stair had been closed for repairs.

"That's cheating."

The Velmor mentor shook his head.

"That's reality. Maps age."

"Then update the map."

"Exactly."

Ava stared at him.

"You did that on purpose."

"Yes."

"I dislike you."

"You learned the lesson."

"I can do both."

At the observatory they were shown weather instruments, star charts and enchanted lenses that revealed air currents as faint moving ribbons. Jasper watched one for a long time. Not because it felt like the Sea. It did not. The moving air was ordinary magic and weather. That was comforting. Not every strange thing needed to be ancient. Calyx came third. Its open afternoon began in the gardens. Harper brightened when she heard they would use magic. Then discovered the assignment was to restore a patch of soil damaged by overcasting.

"This is gardening."

The Calyx mentor, an older boy named Arun, nodded.

"Yes."

"With magic."

"Sometimes."

They tested soil moisture by hand. Added compost. Moved shade cloth. Used only one small spell at the end to encourage root recovery. Ava asked why they did not simply cast first. Arun looked genuinely pleased.

"Because magic is not automatically the best tool just because you can use it."

Harper groaned.

"Every Order is secretly teaching restraint."

"Probably because students keep needing it."

Calyx also ran part of the infirmary support system, greenhouse food production and peer tutoring. Jasper watched two older students help a homesick first-year write a letter without pretending the homesickness was a problem to cure. Calyx felt quiet. Not passive. Busy in the way kitchens were busy. Things growing because people kept showing up. Nocten was last. Everybody expected darkness. Nocten gave them paperwork. Harper nearly left. Their first task involved six witness statements about a broken storage cabinet. Four were accurate. One was mistaken. One was deliberately misleading. The first-years had to determine what most likely happened without accusing anyone based on confidence alone. Ava became terrifying. Jasper watched her compare times. Harper watched people's phrasing. The Nocten mentor nodded.

Jasper noticed the quietest statement included a sound nobody else mentioned. Together they worked out that a shelf bracket had failed before the door was forced, meaning the student everyone suspected had probably only found the damage.

"Useful secrets are not the same as secretive people."

Ava looked up.

"Meaning?"

"Sometimes information should be protected because it belongs to someone. Sometimes it should be shared because withholding it creates danger. The difficult part is knowing which."

Jasper thought of the Deepwater records. Ava did too. He could tell. Harper asked, "Do you all spend your lives doing evidence puzzles?"

"No. Sometimes we have biscuits."

Nocten's common room was the least gloomy place Jasper had expected. Low lamps. Deep chairs. A wall of locked cabinets beside an equally large wall of open books. One notice read: PRIVACY IS NOT THE SAME AS SECRECY. Another: KNOWING SOMETHING DOES NOT ALWAYS GIVE YOU THE RIGHT TO REPEAT IT. Jasper looked at her. Ava stood under that one for a while. Later, as they crossed the courtyard back toward West residence, Harper said, "This is terrible."

"What?"

"They're all good."

Ava nodded.

"That is the point."

"I wanted three of them to be stupid so this would be easy."

"Why would the academy maintain three stupid Orders?"

"Tradition?"

Jasper laughed. The week had done what it was supposed to do. It had not told them where they belonged. It had made belonging harder to predict. That evening the three sat on the floor of Room Fourteen with four Order pamphlets between them. Harper picked up Brannor's.

"Still my favourite."

Ava picked up Nocten's, then Calyx's, then put both down.

"I hate this."

Jasper held Velmor's map. He could imagine himself there. He could imagine himself in Brannor. Calyx. Even Nocten. That should have made him anxious. Instead it helped. Whatever the Choosing Tide did, none of the four platforms meant losing himself. Orders were not cages. They were communities. Harper looked at him.

"You're doing the calm face."

"I'm thinking."

"Dangerous."

Jasper tossed the Velmor pamphlet at her. She caught it.

"Maybe you're Velmor," he said.

Harper looked offended enough to prove the entire point.

CHAPTER SIXTY-NINE
THE FINAL

The first-year handball final became important by accident. It began because rain had cancelled coastal fieldwork. It continued because Theo had made a scoreboard. It became a competition because Harper existed. By late spring, the central courtyard handball squares had their own unofficial league. No magic allowed. No levitating the ball. No slowing someone else's shot. No arguing that a tiny gust of magically created wind "barely counted." That last rule had Harper's name beside it. The final paired Harper and Mia. Not Room Fourteen against another room. Harper against Mia. Jasper had expected Harper to love this. She did. He had not expected her to be nervous. That became obvious when she retied the same shoelace three times. Ava noticed too.

"You know this does not count toward anything."

Harper looked up.

"I know."

"There is no Order consideration."

"I know."

"No marks."

"Ava."

"No permanent record."

"AVA."

Mia was warming up on the opposite square. She had become one of their closest friends outside Room Fourteen over the year. She was not dramatic. She did not charge into things like Harper. She had a dry laugh, fast hands and an irritating ability to place a ball exactly six centimetres from where Harper wanted it.

"You can lose," Jasper said.

Harper stared at him.

"Whose side are you on?"

"Yours."

"Doesn't sound like it."

"You can still lose."

She stood.

"I am going to remember this betrayal."

The courtyard filled during afternoon free period. Not everyone cared about handball. Everyone cared about watching Harper care about handball. Theo had made a sign. HARPER VALE VS MIA SANTOS, Underneath, in smaller writing:

NO MAGIC. WE MEAN IT. The second too. Harper stopped smiling. Harper reached too far. Missed. Three-zero. Harper's shoulders tightened. Jasper knew the pattern. Mia served. Harper smashed the return. Too hard. Out. Four-zero. Mia waited.

Professor Ward volunteered to referee because, as she put it, "I have survived greater administrative risks." The first point went to Mia. Mia sent a low shot across the corner. The crowd made the sound crowds make when they sense a favourite having a terrible afternoon. When Harper felt herself losing, she moved faster. When faster failed, she moved harder. When harder failed, she usually invented a new category of mistake. Harper stared at the line. Ward said nothing. She inhaled. Then laughed once. Jasper expected Harper to argue.

"That was awful."

Mia grinned.

"It was."

Something changed. Harper stopped trying to win the whole match with every hit. She began placing shots. Waiting. Reading Mia. One point. Then another. Mia took one back. Harper stayed steady. Five-two became six-five. Then seven-seven. Ava had stopped pretending not to care.

"Her back corner is open," she muttered.

Jasper looked at her.

"You're coaching?"

"Observing."

"Very loudly."

The final point lasted longer than the rest. Mia sent Harper left. Harper recovered. Harper sent Mia deep. Mia returned. The ball clipped the line. Harper had a chance to smash. Everyone saw it. Old Harper would have. Instead she waited half a beat and tapped the ball short. Mia ran. Reached. The ball touched her fingers and bounced outside the square. Harper won. The courtyard erupted. Harper stood frozen for one second. Then Mia launched herself across the line and hugged her hard enough to nearly knock them both over.

"You absolute pest."

Harper laughed.

"You were four up."

"I noticed."

"I thought I was cooked."

"You were. Then you stopped trying to murder the ball."

Ward handed Harper a tiny wooden trophy somebody had clearly made in workshop class. It leaned slightly left. Harper held it above her head like an international championship. That evening she placed it on the Bag Bed. Jasper noticed she put it beside Ava's library commendation and his storm-safety thank-you card. Not above them. Beside.

"Growth," Ava said.

Harper turned.

"What?"

"Nothing."

The next day, Harper lost to a second-year during open courtyard. She argued one line call for three full minutes. Growth, Jasper decided, was not magic. It did not happen all at once.

CHAPTER SEVENTY
A BOOK WITH NO SEA IN IT

Soren Bell had spent most of first year sitting near Jasper without becoming his friend. That was not an insult. Some friendships began loudly. Harper had nearly crashed into Jasper on arrival day and then behaved as though they had known each other for years by dinner. Ava had introduced herself like a tiny lawyer. Soren appeared more slowly. He sat near Jasper in History. Borrowed erasers. Returned books on time. Once, during winter, he had quietly moved Jasper's wet shoes closer to the drying vent without saying anything. By spring, Jasper knew Soren liked old machinery, hated pears and could imitate Ashcroft's disappointed sigh perfectly. They still rarely spent time alone. That changed because of a book. Soren found it in the mechanical history section. TIDAL LIFTS AND COUNTERWEIGHTS OF COASTAL SCHOOLS.

He carried it to lunch as though it were treasure. Harper looked at the cover.

"That is the worst title I have ever seen."

Soren sat down.

"You're the worst title I've ever seen."

Harper stared. Then slowly smiled.

"I like him."

Soren opened to a fold-out diagram. Tidefall's old harbour lifts had once used tide pressure and weighted shafts to move supplies up the cliff before modern levitation systems existed. Jasper leaned in.

"That's under the west service stair."

Soren nodded.

"Most of it got sealed seventy years ago."

Ava looked over.

"Why?"

"Maintenance cost. Also apparently one platform spent a whole winter going to the wrong floor."

Harper brightened.

"Can we find it?"

Ava and Jasper said, "No," together. Soren looked delighted.

"I wasn't asking."

That afternoon Jasper and Soren went to the library to copy the diagram for the Chronicle appendix. Harper had handball practice. Ava was with the safety-project group. For the first time in weeks, Jasper walked somewhere without either of them. It felt strange for exactly three minutes. Then Soren started explaining counterweights. Jasper forgot to feel strange. They sat by an east window where the Sea was barely visible. Soren drew the old lift shaft. Jasper copied labels. Neither mentioned Water. That was unusual too. Most students who spent time alone with Jasper eventually asked. What did it feel like? Did he hear words? Could he make waves? Did he think the Sea knew their names? Soren asked none of them.

After forty minutes Jasper said, "You can ask." Soren looked up.

"Ask what?"

"About the Sea."

"Why?"
