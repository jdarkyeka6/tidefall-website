"What if it is warning me?"

Thorne did not answer immediately.

"Then you pass the warning to people who can check. As you did."

"And if I'm wrong?"

"They check and find nothing."

"What if people start depending on me?"

That question surprised Thorne. Then pleased him.

"They should not."

Jasper relaxed.

Jasper nodded.

Jasper looked up.

"I'd have to tell the Headmaster he's wrong."

Jasper smiled. Power returned before lunch. The electric lights came back. Harper booed. Ava said she was ridiculous. Tidefall returned to normal. The Sea did not send anything else.

CHAPTER FORTY-THREE
THE OTHER FIRST-YEARS

Jasper realised his world had become too small when Mia said it aloud.

"Do you three ever do anything separately?"

They were eating lunch.

Jasper looked left at Harper.

Then right at Ava.

Then back at Mia.

"Yes."

Mia waited.

Jasper tried to think of an example.

Harper said, "I go to the bathroom alone."

Ava closed her eyes.

Mia laughed.

"I mean everything else. Classes. Lunch. Library. Handball."

Soren looked up from his book. "They live together."

"So do lots of people."

Jasper felt defensive.

Then he wondered if Mia had a point.

Room Fourteen had become a tiny country.

Not intentionally.

Harper and Ava had been beside Jasper during nearly every strange Water event. If Jasper went somewhere, one or both often followed. If Harper found something interesting, she dragged them into it. If Ava made a theory, they heard it first.

There was nothing wrong with being close friends.

Tidefall still contained more than three people.

The next week, Foundations assigned mixed practical groups.

Jasper worked with Mia and Theo.

Harper was paired with Soren and a quiet girl named Leila.

Ava joined three students from East Residence.

Jasper expected his new group to feel wrong.

It did not.

Their task was to maintain a shared shield around a stack of wooden blocks while soft balls rolled toward it from changing directions.

First attempt: all three blocked the same ball and left the other side open.

Second attempt: Theo became distracted.

Third attempt: Mia called positions, Jasper covered the left side and Theo remembered he had a right side.

Success.

Across the hall, Harper's group was doing badly.

Soren wanted instructions before every movement.

Harper wanted everybody to move before instructions existed.

Leila finally said, "Both of you stop talking."

They stopped.

Their next attempt worked.

Ava's group finished first because, according to Ava, somebody else had a better formation idea and she had allowed them to use it.

Harper stared at her afterward.

"You let someone else plan?"

"Yes."

"Are you ill?"

At lunch, everybody complained about their groups.

Nobody stopped being friends.

That mattered.

By the end of the week, Jasper had joined Theo for handball without Harper.

Harper spent an hour practising with Mia.

Ava went to a Nocten puzzle session with Leila.

The change felt obvious once it happened.

It had not felt obvious before.

Jasper also learned something else.

The Water Titan did not care whether Harper and Ava were standing beside him.

One afternoon, he felt a brief pressure while walking with Theo.

Theo noticed Jasper stop.

"What?"

Jasper hesitated.

Then remembered how secrets grew when nobody was allowed to name them.

"I get a strange feeling from the sea sometimes."

Theo stared. "Seasick?"

"No."

Jasper gave him the short version.

Teachers knew.

Sometimes Jasper sensed something unusual.

He could not control waves.

Theo listened.

"That's weird."

"Yeah."

"Cool though."

"Sometimes."

Theo looked toward the western windows. "Can you make waves?"

Jasper's stomach tightened.

There it was again.

The easiest wrong idea.

"No."

"Not at all?"

"No. It isn't mine."

Theo nodded.

"Okay."

That was it.

No disappointment.

No argument.

Jasper had expected people to want the exciting version.

Some did.

Some accepted the true one.

It isn't mine.

He liked the sentence.

That night he told Harper and Ava.

Ava wrote it inside the notebook cover.

Harper read it.

IT ISN'T MINE.

"Good title for your life," she said.

Jasper threw a sock at her.

CHAPTER FORTY-FOUR
THE LETTER THAT CAME BACK

Thorne received a reply from Deepwater's former student in October. Jasper learned this because Thorne told him. That mattered more than the reply itself. The Headmaster asked Jasper to come to his study after dinner. Harper and Ava came too, after Jasper asked. Thorne did not object. A single envelope lay on his desk. No name visible.

"Months ago," Thorne said, "I told you we had attempted contact with people connected to Deepwater. One replied."

Jasper's heart sped up.

"The student?"

"Yes."

"Can I read it?"

Thorne looked at the envelope.

"No. It is private."

Disappointment hit hard. Jasper nodded anyway. Thorne continued.

"They gave me permission to share three things with you."

Jasper sat straighter.

"First: they do not wish to speak about the event directly or return to Tidefall."

Harper nodded.

"Fair."

"Second: they believe what they experienced was communication, not control. They are certain now that the sea's responses were independent choices."

Jasper's skin prickled.

"Certain?"

"Their word."

Ava asked, "Based on what?"

"They did not give permission for the details."

Ava looked frustrated but accepted it.

"Third," Thorne said, "they asked me to give Jasper one sentence exactly."

The room went quiet. Jasper waited. Thorne read from a small note.

"If it answers you, do not mistake being heard for being obeyed."

Jasper stopped breathing for a second. Harper looked at him. Ava's eyes moved to the Map of Things That Should Not Happen sitting partly out of her bag. Jasper repeated the sentence silently. Being heard. Being obeyed. Different. He had known the idea. Hearing it from the one person who had made the mistake turned it into something heavier.

"Are they okay?" Jasper asked.

Thorne considered.

"They have a life. A family. Work. They are not frozen at the worst thing that happened when they were young."

Jasper was relieved. He had imagined the former student as a warning more than a person. That had been unfair. Harper said, "Tell them thanks." Thorne nodded.

"I will."

Ava asked, "Do they still feel the sea?" Thorne looked at the envelope.

"That was not one of the things they gave permission to share."

Ava sighed.

"Sorry."

"Curiosity is not a crime. Respecting the answer matters."

They left the study. Outside, the corridor windows reflected night. Harper said, "That's creepy." Jasper looked at her.

"What?"

"Not bad creepy. Like... you're not the first."

"We knew that."

"Knowing from a file isn't the same as someone sending you a sentence."

Ava nodded. Jasper agreed. The historical communicator was no longer ancient history. They were alive somewhere. They had once stood at Tidefall as a child and felt something in the sea. They had made mistakes. They had grown up. That possibility mattered too. Jasper was seven. The Sea mystery felt enormous. But it would not be his entire life unless he let it become one. Back in Room Fourteen, Ava opened her notebook. Under KNOWN FACTS she added: BEING HEARD ≠ BEING OBEYED. Jasper did not object. Harper drew a line under it.

CHAPTER FORTY-FIVE
THE STORM CALENDAR

November brought storms.

Not every day, and not always the dramatic kind. Tidefall could begin a morning under blue sky and finish dinner with rain striking the western windows hard enough to drown out conversation.

The Academy treated weather like another timetable.

A green flag meant normal coastal access.

Yellow meant supervised access only.

Red closed the external cliff paths and lower harbour routes to students.

Harper checked the flag every breakfast.

"Because weather is interesting," she insisted.

Ava did not believe her.

Jasper watched too, but for a different reason.

Storms changed the Water Titan's presence.

Sometimes the pressure behind his ribs grew stronger. Sometimes it became distant enough that he wondered whether he had imagined it. On other days the physical force of the sea and the strange awareness beneath it became almost impossible to separate.

Professor Elowen refused to let anyone turn that confusion into a neat pattern.

"Wind speed is measured," she told the class. "Wave height is measured. Jasper's experience is reported separately. We do not combine two kinds of evidence simply because we would like them to match."

Ava looked pleased.

Jasper liked the rule for a different reason. It meant he did not have to be correct every time he felt something.

One Tuesday, the weather flag turned yellow before lunch. Cloud thickened offshore and Ocean Studies moved indoors. Jasper felt the Titan for most of the afternoon, broad and restless but not angry.

Nothing happened.

Wednesday was green.

No presence.

Thursday was red.

The presence returned.

Friday was clear, and it stayed.

Ava's first attempt at a pattern died immediately.

She looked at her notebook for a long time.

"Choice," she said finally.

Harper stared. "You said that without getting angry."

"Growth."

The Academy also began its storm drills.

First-years practised moving from exposed wings to internal halls, following whichever route staff marked that day. Older students rehearsed harbour closures. Emergency blankets, water supplies and safe assembly rooms stopped being mysterious cupboards and became ordinary parts of school.

Harper liked the bells.

Ava liked the procedures.

Jasper hated the first drill.

The continuous alarm sounded too much like the one after Harper's fall, and his body reacted before his brain remembered there was no emergency. His heart raced. His hands went cold.

Nobody noticed except Harper.

She stood beside him until the drill ended.

No speech.

No joke.

Just there.

The second drill was easier.

By the third, Jasper could hear the alarm without seeing the broken ledge in his head.

Tidefall prepared seriously because storms were normal on the coast, not because anyone expected ancient magic to attack the harbour.

Then Professor Crowe found damage on the outer breakwater.

It was not dangerous yet. Winter swells had shifted one old stone section by several centimetres, and maintenance crews began work immediately. Temporary beams appeared along the harbour edge. A work barge was moored near the outer gate. New ropes ran through old guide rings built into the stone.

Ocean Studies watched the repairs from the upper gallery.

Jasper felt the Water Titan strongly whenever the crews worked near the gate.

"Could be the noise," Ava suggested.

"Could be the wards," Jasper said.

Harper looked through the glass. "Could hate construction."

Ava wrote all three possibilities.

During a supervised engineering lesson, Crowe showed them how the breakwater worked.

"It does not stop the sea," she said. "It changes where the energy goes. If you try to make water stop existing, you lose."

Harper looked at Jasper.

Jasper looked back.

Crowe noticed.

"Yes," she said. "Engineering becomes philosophical by accident sometimes."

The repairs were expected to take three weeks.

One of the temporary mooring ropes passed through an old stone guide ring near the gate. Jasper saw it and remembered an impression from the Spring Fair: wood against stone, a rope snapping tight, water forcing through a narrow entrance.

He told Crowe.

She checked the rope herself.

New rope.

Sound ring.

Nothing visibly wrong.

"Good," Jasper said.

Crowe nodded. "Good."

Not every strange image needed to predict disaster.

Two nights later, Jasper dreamed about the same rope.

In the dream it was underwater, pulled sideways until the gate jammed half-open. Dark water forced through the gap.

He woke at three in the morning with the Titan's presence heavy behind his ribs.

Harper woke when he sat up.

"What?"

"Dream."

Ava stirred almost immediately.

Jasper told them.

They woke Mae, who woke Professor Ward, who contacted the harbour staff. A check found the gate working normally. The rope was secure. Nothing was jammed.

Jasper felt stupid.

Ward would not let him.

"You reported something unusual. Staff checked. Everything is safe."

"What if it was nothing?"

"Then the system worked."

Jasper frowned.

Ward pulled the blanket back over his knees. "Especially if it was nothing."

A week later, during a routine test in clear weather, the outer gate jammed.

The temporary work rope had shifted sideways under load exactly the way Jasper had seen in the dream.

Staff stopped the test within minutes. Nobody was in danger.

When the old guide ring was removed, a hairline crack appeared along its inner edge. The damage had been too small to see until pressure opened it.

Crowe placed the ring on a workbench.

Jasper stared at it.

"Useful warning," she said.

He thought of Thorne's rule.

Not an alarm system.

"Maybe."

Crowe nodded.

"Good answer."

Outside, the weather flag remained green.

CHAPTER FORTY-SIX
THE LAST QUIET WEEK

The last quiet week before the storm began with pancakes. Jasper remembered this later because disaster stories always seemed to forget breakfast. Tidefall served pancakes on Sunday once a month. Harper ate five. Ava said this was excessive. Harper said the academy had provided five, therefore the academy supported the decision. Foundations introduced moving shields. Jasper loved it.

Jasper ate four and stayed out of the argument. The week that followed felt normal enough to be suspicious only in hindsight. Instead of placing a barrier and holding it, students learned to shift the resistance field while maintaining shape. A shield did not need to be huge if it could be where danger arrived. Professor Ward set soft balls rolling around a circular track while students redirected them away from targets. Jasper finished with only two hits. Harper finished with one because she physically ducked under another and argued that this counted. Ava finished with three because she overthought the first pattern and then adapted.

"Acceptable," she said.

Harper nearly fainted.

"You said acceptable about your own imperfect result."

"Please stop."

"I am witnessing history."

Making and Mending taught emergency knots reinforced with simple magic. Ocean Studies reviewed storm harbour procedure. Order orientation continued. The Choosing ceremony was now only six weeks away. Harper's certainty about Brannor had become a running joke.

"What if Velmor?" Mia asked.

"No."

"Calyx?"

"No."

"Nocten?"

"I would walk out."

Ava said, "You cannot refuse."

"Watch me."

Jasper laughed. Nobody knew where Ava would go. Nocten seemed obvious until she spent a Calyx session organising a greenhouse irrigation system and loved it. Brannor seemed possible after she became the best planner in rescue exercises. Velmor liked her mapping skills. Ava hated everyone guessing. Jasper remained equally unclear. One older student said Brannor because of shielding. Another said Velmor because he kept reaching toward the unknown. A Nocten prefect said curiosity mattered more than secrecy. A Calyx teacher said listening was a kind of patience. All four fit. The Tide would decide. Mostly. He wrote home. He played handball.

Jasper stopped caring which one sounded most like him. That thought used to bother him. Now it mostly felt like waiting for a class list. The Titan-presence was quiet all week. Jasper almost forgot about it for entire mornings. He helped Soren repair a book cover. Harper and Mia tried to build a practice obstacle course in the common room and were stopped before furniture died. Ava spent two hours making a revision schedule for exams that did not start for a month. Jasper told her this was unhealthy. She added him to the schedule anyway. On Thursday, Room Fourteen cleaned Bag Bed properly for the first time. Choosing Tide meant first-years would move into permanent Order accommodation after summer. Their temporary rooms would eventually belong to someone else.

Mae said the residence would conduct end-of-year inventory soon. West-stair pebble: keep. Old Order pamphlets: recycle. Brass washer: keep. Paper boat: keep. Bag Bed could not remain a museum. The three sat around it deciding what to keep. Broken handball: Harper wanted keep. Ava said rubbish. Vote: two to one keep. Dried Calyx leaf: Ava keep. Unknown sock: finally thrown away after nobody claimed it for nine months. Harper held a faded blue cloth square.

"First magic."

Jasper recognised it. One-centimetre exercise.

"How did you get that?"

"It was in my bag."

Ava stared.

"You stole school equipment."

"Accidentally collected history."

"Return it."

Harper put it in the return pile. Painfully. Jasper found his mother's first note. ONE WEEK UNTIL YOUR FIRST PROPER LETTER. I AM COUNTING. He kept that. Harper lay across it. By evening Bag Bed could technically become a bed again.

"We fixed it."

Ava said, "You are the current obstruction."

"Temporary."

Jasper looked around Room Fourteen. The thought of leaving it felt strange. This had been home before Orders. No Tide had chosen them here. No magic decided they belonged together. Staff had placed three names on one room list. The rest had happened because they kept choosing to come back to one another. Jasper liked that better than destiny.
