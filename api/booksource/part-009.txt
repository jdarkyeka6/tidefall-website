. Ava tried to erase it. Harper protected the drawing with both hands. Some mysteries were easier than others.

CHAPTER TWENTY-THREE
DETENTION WITHOUT A CRIME

Harper received detention for not breaking a rule. She considered this proof Tidefall's justice system had failed. It happened after evening study. A Year Two student dared her to climb onto the low roof above the covered walkway. Harper said no. This was true.

Then she went to see how they had climbed up. A prefect found her standing beside the maintenance ladder. She had not touched it. She had also brought Jasper and Ava because, in Harper's words, witnesses were important. The prefect asked why they were there. Harper said, "Research." This was not a good answer. Mae listened to the story in the common room.

"You did not climb?"

"No."

"You did not cross the staff chain?"

"No."

"You walked across the academy after evening study to inspect how someone else broke a rule?"

Harper hesitated.

"Yes."

Mae sighed.

"You are not in detention for climbing. You are in detention for ignoring evening residence boundaries and following a dare situation instead of telling someone."

Harper crossed her arms.

"That is a different crime."

"Correct."

Jasper and Ava received the same consequence. One hour Saturday morning helping the grounds staff clean storm debris from the inner courtyard. Ava accepted this immediately. Jasper thought it was fair. Harper complained until Saturday. Then discovered the groundskeeper let them use long grabbers to collect branches and stopped complaining. The work was cold but not terrible. A winter storm the night before had filled the courtyard with leaves, twigs and one entire gull nest nobody knew how to relocate. The groundskeeper, Mr Kellan, eventually placed the nest on a sheltered roof ledge. Harper asked how it had fallen.

"Wind."

"Did the birds leave?"

"Probably before the storm."

"Will they come back?"

"Maybe."

Harper spent the next ten minutes worrying about gulls she had previously fed against school rules. Jasper found this funny and did not say so. Halfway through detention, a branch had jammed against the drain beside the western wall. Jasper used the grabber to pull it free. Water rushed into the grate. For a second he felt a pressure from below. Not Sea. Residue? He stopped. Ava saw.

"What kind?"

Jasper had learned her question now.

"Not sure."

Mr Kellan looked over.

"Everything all right?"

Jasper explained. Mr Kellan crouched by the drain.

"This run goes down to the old cliff cistern. Picks up rainwater and dumps through a channel above the south cove."

Ava said, "Marine-connected."

"Eventually."

Jasper touched the stone beside the grate. No pressure.

"Gone."

Mr Kellan shrugged.

"Old academy. Water everywhere."

Then he returned to branches. Jasper liked that answer too. Old academy. Water everywhere. Not every pipe needed mythology. After detention, the trio bought hot chocolate from the dining hall kitchen window using their weekly snack tokens. Harper held hers in both hands.

"Worst punishment ever."

Ava looked at her.

"You enjoyed it."

"I enjoyed parts against my will."

Jasper said, "Maybe don't inspect crime ladders after curfew."

"It wasn't a crime ladder."

"You called it a crime."

"The crime was different."

They sat in a window alcove watching rain return. Jasper thought about how easily Harper could have climbed the ladder. A month earlier, she might have. She had not. Progress rarely looked dramatic while it happened. Sometimes it looked like three children doing detention for a worse choice they did not make.

CHAPTER TWENTY-FOUR
THE STORY THORNE WOULD NOT FINISH

Ava requested an appointment with the Headmaster. Harper said this was frightening behaviour for a seven-year-old. Ava said appointments existed to be requested.

"What are you asking him?" Jasper said.

"Deepwater."

They were crossing the upper library gallery at the time. Jasper stopped beside the rail.

"Why now?"

Ava held up her notebook. "Because we know enough to misunderstand it." That sounded exactly like Professor Elowen. Thorne agreed to see all three after lunch on Thursday. His office occupied a round room overlooking both the inland grounds and the western sea. Jasper had expected shelves full of mysterious objects. Instead there were books, maps, two badly organised stacks of paperwork and a mug that said WORLD'S LEAST AVAILABLE HEADMASTER. Harper read it aloud. Thorne said, "A gift from staff."

"Accurate?"

"Painfully."

Ava opened the meeting with a list of facts. Deepwater happened twenty-three years ago. A student attempted to influence unusual marine magic. Another student was injured. The lower docks closed for twelve days. Safety rules changed afterward. Some related records involved older Selari material.

"What are we missing?" she asked.

Thorne looked at the three of them for a long moment.

"Context."

"Which is why we're here," Harper said.

Ava closed her eyes briefly. Thorne did not seem offended. "The student involved had an unusual sensitivity to the coastal currents around Tidefall. Not identical to yours, Jasper, but close enough that the comparison matters." Jasper leaned forward.

"Could they communicate?"

"They believed they could."

The wording returned again.

"Did you know them?" Ava asked.

Thorne's gaze moved to the window. That was answer enough before he spoke.

"Yes."

The room became quieter.

"Were you a student?" Jasper asked.

"A young staff member."

"What happened?"

Thorne folded his hands. "They became convinced that sensing intention meant they could direct it. They began testing responses. Small ones first. Timing. Waves. Currents near the harbour." Harper frowned. "And it worked?"

"Sometimes."

That was the dangerous answer.

"Enough to make them believe the failures were their own mistakes," Thorne continued. "During Deepwater, they attempted something larger. The water responded differently from what they expected. Another student was injured."

Jasper felt cold.

"Did they survive?"

Thorne paused.

"Yes."

"Where are they now?"

The pause lasted longer.

"That part is not mine to tell you."

Harper leaned forward. "Why?"

"Because being part of Academy history does not remove someone's right to privacy."

Ava nodded slowly. Jasper understood the answer and hated it anyway. Before they left, Thorne looked directly at him.

"Promise me something. If you ever feel tempted to test whether the thing you sense will obey you, tell an adult first."

Jasper glanced at Harper and Ava.

"And them," Harper said.

"Preferably both," Thorne replied.

Outside the office, Harper put her hand into the middle of the corridor. Ava stared at it. "What are you doing?"

"Promise stack."

"That is not a thing."

Jasper placed his hand on Harper's anyway. Ava sighed and added hers.

"Tell an adult," she said.

"And us," Harper added.

Jasper nodded. "Okay." The appointment was granted for Wednesday after dinner. Thorne's study was less impressive than rumour suggested. No wall of magical weapons. No hidden dragon. Books, maps, desk, two tall windows and a kettle. Harper looked disappointed. Thorne added a third chair.

"One evening they went to the lower docks with another student. They attempted a stronger contact. The sea surged unexpectedly. The second student was thrown against the platform and injured. The first nearly went into the water. Staff intervened."

"This is why I tell you not to test. Not because I think the sea is waiting to attack you. Not because I think you are repeating someone else's life. Because response can become addictive when you do not understand what is responding."

"You ask a question and receive something impossible. Then you want to know if it was real. So you ask again. Then you want to know if you caused it. So you ask for something specific. Eventually you stop listening and start demanding proof."

Harper nodded seriously. The meeting ended twenty minutes later. On the way back, Jasper felt lighter and heavier at once. They knew more. Not a name for whatever waited in the sea. Not a grand explanation. Only a fact: Someone before him had experienced something similar. They had mistaken communication for command. People got hurt. Harper stopped at the residence door. He meant it. That mattered later.

CHAPTER TWENTY-FIVE
THE DAY NOTHING ANSWERED

For eleven days, the presence disappeared.

Jasper did not notice on the first day.

Or the second.

By the fourth, he noticed.

By the seventh, he was counting.

By the eleventh, he hated that he was counting.

Tidefall contained water everywhere. Jasper saw it from Ocean Studies, western windows, high stairs and courtyard walls. Every time, the sea behaved like sea.

Waves.

Wind.

Foam.

No pressure behind his ribs.

No strange images.

No sense of being noticed.

Jasper told himself this was good.

Thorne wanted him not to chase answers.

His mother wanted him safe.

Harper wanted him not to become what she had started calling Weird Dock Boy.

Ava wanted accurate information, although she had stopped asking him every morning whether anything had happened.

Jasper wanted the presence to come back.

That was the problem.

His mind began inventing reasons for the silence.

Maybe talking to Thorne had changed something.

Maybe the presence had moved away from Tidefall.

Maybe the weather was different.

Then came the worst possibility.

Maybe there had never been anything there at all.

Perhaps Jasper's own magic had made water behave strangely.

Perhaps the dreams were only dreams.

Perhaps the lower door had opened because of an old ward.

Perhaps he had wanted a mystery badly enough to build one from ordinary things.

He did not tell Harper or Ava that thought.

During library period, Jasper was supposed to find a book on basic shield geometry.

Instead, he found himself reading old coastal logs.

He told himself he was only checking one reference.

Then another.

One note from twenty years earlier read:

UNUSUAL STILLNESS REPORTED AT WEST WALL. NO STUDENT INVOLVEMENT CONFIRMED.

That should have reassured him.

It did not.

The library window faced east.

Jasper wanted to see the west wall.

He left.

That was the part he knew was wrong before he did it.

Founders' Courtyard was allowed during free period. Jasper did not cross any barrier or go near the lower docks.

He also did not tell anyone where he was going.

The afternoon was cold and clear.

Jasper stood several metres behind the western wall.

The sea rolled below.

Normal.

He closed his eyes.

Nothing.

Opened them.

Normal.

He tried to notice the place inside his chest where the pressure usually appeared.

Nothing.

Then he thought one sentence.

If you're there, notice me.

Still nothing.

Disappointment hurt more than Jasper expected.

"You promised."

He spun around.

Harper stood beneath the arch.

Ava was beside her.

Jasper's face became hot.

"I'm not doing anything."

Ava's expression was worse than anger.

"You left the library alone and came here because it has been quiet."

Jasper stared at her. "How do you know?"

"Because you keep looking at the sea."

Harper stepped closer.

"Were you testing it?"

"No."

The answer came too quickly.

Harper stopped.

Jasper looked away.

"I just wanted to know if it was still there."

Ava said quietly, "That is testing."

"I didn't ask it to move."

"You asked for a response."

The sea struck the cliff below.

Ordinary.

Jasper's throat tightened.

"Maybe there isn't anything."

Harper's anger disappeared.

"What?"

"Maybe I made it up."

Ava came closer.

"You don't believe that."

"I don't know."

There it was.

The real problem.

Jasper did not want to control the sea.

He wanted certainty.

Harper sat on the courtyard step.

"I saw the wave flatten."

"Weather can do weird things."

"I saw the lower door."

"Could have been a ward."

Ava said, "I have records that do not match normal tide behaviour."

"Records can be wrong."

Ava almost looked offended.

Then she understood.

"There will always be another explanation if you need one."

Jasper looked at her.

"That isn't bad."

"No," Ava said. "But you cannot make something prove itself to you just because not knowing feels horrible."

Harper looked toward the sea.

"What if it never answers again?"

Jasper had not let himself ask that.

"I don't know."

"Then we still live here."

She pointed toward Tidefall.

"You still have homework. You are still bad at handball. Ava still spies on us with notebooks."

"Observation," Ava corrected.

Jasper laughed weakly.

Harper looked at him. "You don't need the water thing to make your life interesting."

"I know."

"Good. Because you are not that interesting."

Jasper shoved her shoulder.

She shoved back.

Ava stepped between them before friendship became a courtyard incident.

They told Professor Elowen exactly what Jasper had done.

She listened.

Then asked, "What consequence makes sense?"

Jasper stared at the floor.

"No coastal free periods alone for a week?"

Elowen nodded. "Agreed. Indoor study room during free periods."

Harper whispered, "Self-issued detention."

Elowen looked at her.

Harper immediately became silent.

For a week, Jasper stayed inside.

The presence did not return.

On the eighth day, he crossed a western gallery with Harper and Ava while talking about homework.

He was not looking for anything.

A cool pressure brushed his chest.

Brief.

Soft.

There.

Jasper stopped.

Then it vanished.

He began laughing.

Harper stared. "What?"

"It noticed."

Ava smiled.

"After you stopped asking."

Jasper nodded.

He did not call it back.

CHAPTER TWENTY-SIX
THE OBSERVATION LEDGE

Harper fell on a Tuesday afternoon.

Nothing about breakfast suggested the day would become important.

Porridge.

Toast.

Soren spilling juice on a library book and nearly crying until Ava helped dry it.

Foundations involved holding two beads at once. Harper managed eleven seconds and declared herself a genius.

By lunch, Jasper had forgotten to write home on Sunday and was trying to fix this before his mother noticed.

The sky was pale blue.

The wind was light.

Ocean Studies should have been boring.

Professor Elowen led the first-years to a higher observation ledge north of their usual terrace. The platform was broad and protected by a stone barrier, with a painted safety line several metres back from the outer wall.

Staff checked the surface before the lesson began.

Students stayed inside the line.

Their task was coastline mapping.

Harper hated it.

"It keeps being rock."

Ava did not look up from her page. "That is generally what cliffs are."

"Could change."

"Please don't ask it to."

Jasper smiled and tried to draw the curve of a narrow inlet.

He had become better at not staring at the sea every few seconds.

That afternoon, he barely looked down.

The first warning was a small crunch.

Grit beneath a shoe.

Jasper looked up.

Harper stood a short distance away, still inside the safety line, leaning sideways to compare her drawing with the cliff.

Her right foot slipped.

It should have been nothing.

There was plenty of stone behind the barrier.

Harper threw one arm out.

Her other shoe landed on loose gravel.

That slipped too.

Her body went backward.

Several children shouted.

Professor Elowen moved.

Jasper moved.

Everybody moved too late.

Harper hit the ground hard enough to lose her breath.

Momentum carried her across the sloping stone.

She reached for the surface.

Missed.

Near the outer barrier, winter runoff had washed grit into a shallow channel beneath the lower gap between two old posts.

Harper slid into it.

Then through it.

She disappeared over the outer side.

For one second, Jasper's mind refused to understand what his eyes had seen.

Then he reached the wall.

Open air.

A narrow rock shelf sat several metres below.

Harper struck it on one side.

Rolled.

Slid again.

Then there was nothing beneath her.

The pressure behind Jasper's ribs arrived so hard it stole everything else.

Not pain.

Presence.

Huge.

Aware.

Jasper had no time to think a request.

No time to form a spell.

No time to do anything.

The ocean moved.

A swell rose directly beneath the cliff where no ordinary swell should have formed.

It did not look gentle.

It looked powerful enough to break stone.

But it rose at exactly the right moment.

Harper hit the water before she reached the normal sea surface.

White foam swallowed her.

The surge took the speed out of the fall and carried her sideways toward a lower staff platform built into the cliff.

Harper landed hard on wet stone and rolled onto her side.

She did not move.

Jasper stopped breathing.

Professor Elowen was already running toward the emergency stair.

Another teacher blew the continuous alarm.

"Behind the line! Everybody back!"

Jasper gripped the stone wall.

Then Harper coughed.

She moved one arm.

Tried to sit up.

Elowen reached her with two staff members and immediately made her lie back down.

Even from above, Jasper could tell Harper was arguing.

That helped.

Ava stood beside him.

Her face had gone white.

"It moved," Jasper said.

Ava looked at the water.

The strange swell was gone.

The sea had returned to ordinary waves.

"I know."

Teachers moved the first-years into an indoor classroom while Harper was brought up by the protected lower route.

Jasper did not want to leave the ledge.

A staff member put one hand on his shoulder.

"She is awake. Let the adults work."

Jasper obeyed.

Inside, nobody spoke normally.

Mia sat beside him.

"She'll be okay."

Jasper nodded.

He could still feel the presence below Tidefall.

Not as strong.

Still there.

Ava's hands were shaking.

Jasper had never seen that before.

He stared at his own hands.

He had not cast anything.

He had not asked.

Whatever had moved the sea had chosen to act on its own.

Harper was alive because of that choice.

Jasper should have felt relieved.
