CHAPTER THIRTY-SIX
THREE SPELLS AT ONCE

By spring, Professor Elowen stopped giving first-years one magical task at a time.

"Real situations do not wait politely while you finish one thing," she said.

She placed three objects on each table.

A candle.

A wooden disk.

A strip of cloth.

"Keep the light burning. Hold the disk in the air. Move the cloth ten centimetres."

Harper stared.

"At the same time?"

"Yes."

"That is rude."

"Life often is."

The exercise was difficult for a reason Jasper had not expected.

Doing three effects did not mean using three times as much Essence.

It meant dividing attention without pouring too much into any one task.

Jasper lit the candle and raised the disk.

The moment he focused on the cloth, the tiny thread of Essence feeding the flame disappeared.

Darkness.

Ava kept the candle and disk perfect but moved the cloth nine centimetres.

She looked personally offended.

Harper sent too much Essence into the candle.

It flared bright enough to light half the room while the disk shot sideways.

Elowen caught it.

"Less."

Harper groaned.

They practised for weeks.

Jasper eventually discovered that he did better if he thought about where each effect needed to remain rather than constantly pushing it.

Light stays.

Disk stays.

Cloth ends there.

He corrected only when something drifted.

Elowen noticed.

"Good. Maintain without gripping."

The sentence caught Jasper's attention.

Without gripping.

Deepwater had been about somebody mistaking response for control.

Different lesson.

Same shape.

Elowen followed his glance toward the window.

"Sometimes," she said.

Harper solved the exercise differently.

She built a rhythm.

Light.

Disk.

Cloth.

Check.

Ava's breakthrough came when she stopped restarting after tiny errors.

"Eight point eight centimetres," she muttered after one attempt.

Harper looked over. "Nobody cares."

"I care."

"Care less."

"That is not how care works."

"Try."

By the end of the month, all three could complete the exercise more often than they failed.

The rest of spring grew warmer around them.

Courtyards reopened fully.

Handball continued until dinner bells.

Harper beat Mia three games in a row and became unbearable for twenty-four hours.

Mia beat her five times the next day.

Balance returned.

Bag Bed also became impossible.

It held a broken handball, two Order pamphlets, letters from home, library books, a dried Calyx leaf, an old brass washer, a paper boat and one sock that nobody had claimed for months.

Ava tried to create categories.

Harper rejected government interference.

Mae stood in the doorway.

"Within reason ended several months ago."

They were ordered to clean it.

They removed exactly three objects.

"Progress," Ava said.

Jasper received a spring parcel from home with clothes, sunscreen and a newspaper clipping about a whale seen north of Perth.

He studied the photograph longer than necessary.

The whale looked nothing like the shape from his dream.

That should not have needed checking.

He checked anyway.

The Water Titan remained quiet for most of spring.

Sometimes Jasper felt a brief brush of awareness.

He reported it.

Nothing followed.

He began learning that presence did not mean danger.

That lesson was harder than splitting Essence three ways.

One afternoon, Jasper completed the candle, disk and cloth exercise perfectly.

Harper looked at the three objects.

"We are basically professionals."

Ava said, "We can move a cloth while holding a disk."

"Professional cloth movers."

Jasper leaned back against the warm stone outside the classroom.

Across the courtyard, an older student flicked one hand. Six books rose from a bench, stacked themselves and followed at shoulder height.

Harper stared.

"I want that."

"Several years of practice," Ava said.

"I want several years now."

Jasper laughed.

For once, he wanted ordinary magic more than ancient answers.

That felt healthy.

CHAPTER THIRTY-SEVEN
THE DEEPWATER FILE

Ava's request for access to the Deepwater records was three pages long. Harper called it a complaint. Ava called it "properly cited." Professor Crowe called it surprisingly difficult to refuse.

That was how Jasper, Harper and Ava ended up in the Academy archive on a wet Thursday afternoon with Crowe sitting at the same table and two locked doors between them and the public library.

"You are receiving an educational extract," Crowe told them. "Private names and medical information stay sealed. You are seven, and some records are restricted for good reasons."

Harper looked at the thin folder. "Does it still have the interesting bits?" Crowe considered this. "It has the useful bits." Ava looked delighted. The folder was labelled: DEEPWATER REVIEW  
RESTRICTED EXTRACT FOR EDUCATIONAL ACCESS Jasper had expected a story. He found paperwork.

Maintenance reports. Tide charts. Witness statements. Lists of damaged equipment. Notes about a lower-dock closure that lasted twelve days. New age restrictions. New emergency ropes. A recommendation that students never conduct unsupervised experiments involving unexplained marine magic. Deepwater stopped sounding like a legend almost immediately. It had dates. It had repairs. It had somebody's handwriting in the margin saying REPLACE WEST GUIDE RING. Harper read one page twice. "People got hurt because somebody was trying to make the sea do things?"

"That is the simplest version," Crowe said.

Ava looked up. "And the inaccurate version?" Crowe gave her a long look that suggested this was exactly why Ava had been allowed into the room.

"The student involved believed the sea sometimes responded to them. At first, the responses were small enough to look convincing. A current shifted after a request. A surge arrived later than expected. Waves weakened once. Adults observed some of it."

Jasper's stomach tightened.

"They could talk to it?"

"We do not know what they could do," Crowe said. "That distinction matters."

She turned to the oldest page in the folder. Unlike the others, it was a translation. The original Selari text had been copied beside it in faded black ink. Jasper recognised one word immediately.

TAIRA.

The word Professor Ilyan had refused to translate. Beneath it, somebody had written several possible English meanings over the years. The oldest notes were crossed out. Another translation had a question mark beside it. The newest line read:

TAIRA: TITAN.

Jasper stared at the page. Harper leaned over his shoulder. "Titan?" The word was familiar in the ordinary way. Old myths. Huge things. Stories about mountains moving or storms having names. Jasper had never connected it to Tidefall. Crowe did not smile.

"In the oldest Selari records, taira is the word usually translated as Titan. Tidefall history uses it for a small number of ancient beings connected to forces of the natural world."

Ava's pencil stopped.

"Are they real?"

Crowe folded her hands. "That question has several answers depending on which historian you annoy." Harper frowned. "That's not an answer."

"No. It is the reason the subject is difficult."

Jasper looked at the next Selari word.

TAIRU.

Beside it: to sense; to attend toward; to commune with a Titan. Then:

TAIREN: Titan presence / Titan bond.

His skin prickled. The pressure behind his ribs had a word. Maybe. Crowe tapped the page before Jasper could decide too much.

"These translations do not prove what you are experiencing. They prove that people at Tidefall have described experiences that sound similar."

Ava nodded slowly.

"Evidence, not conclusion."

"Exactly."

The old translation contained one sentence underlined so many times the paper had almost worn through.

TO HEAR IS NOT TO RULE.

Jasper read it twice. The Deepwater student had apparently read it too. That had not stopped them. The file described several apparent successes. Enough to build confidence. Enough for a child to believe that being answered meant being obeyed. Then came the experiment that went wrong: a surge entered the inner dock basin, one student fell, another was injured, staff intervened and Tidefall rewrote half its lower-harbour rules. Harper stopped joking.

"So Titans are dangerous."

Crowe shook her head. "Water is dangerous. Cliffs are dangerous. Magic can be dangerous. People can be dangerous when they decide uncertainty is permission to experiment. Do not turn one old file into a monster story." Jasper appreciated that more than he could explain.

"What was the Titan?" he asked.

Crowe looked toward the locked archive shelves.

"That is where the records become less certain. Water, perhaps. Sea, perhaps. Something older than the Academy's categories."

Jasper thought of his dream: black coastline, enormous waves, stone under impossible pressure, waiting so long that years had stopped being useful. For months he had called the presence the Sea because he needed something to call it. Now there was another possibility. Water Titan. The words felt too large. On the walk back to Room Fourteen, Harper was quiet for almost an entire staircase. Then she said, "So your weird water thing might be an ancient Titan."

"Might."

"And you can maybe hear it."

"Maybe."

"And you are not going to try ordering it."

Jasper looked at her.

"No."

"Good."

Ava opened her notebook the moment they reached the room and added a line beneath KNOWN FACTS:

SIMILAR HISTORICAL EXPERIENCES HAVE BEEN CALLED TITAN-SENSE.

Then, after thinking for several seconds, she added another.

THIS IS STILL NOT PROOF.

Harper took the pencil and wrote beneath both:

DO NOT BOSS AROUND THE OCEAN.

Ava stared at her. Jasper left it there.

CHAPTER THIRTY-EIGHT
THE DAY JASPER FAILED EVERYTHING

Jasper knew it was going to be a bad day before breakfast was finished.

There was no nightmare, no fever and no strange pressure from the sea. He was simply tired, and everything he touched seemed determined to prove it.

He dropped his spoon into his cereal.

At Foundations, the three-effect exercise that had worked perfectly the previous week fell apart almost immediately. Jasper lit the candle, raised the wooden disk and tried to move the strip of cloth at the same time. The candle went out. The disk hit the table. The cloth did not move at all.

Professor Elowen looked at the mess. "Reset."

Jasper reset.

The second attempt was worse.

Harper leaned over and whispered, "It's fine."

"I know," Jasper snapped.

She stopped talking.

That made him feel worse than failing.

By the fourth attempt, frustration had settled hot behind his eyes. He could feel himself pushing too much Essence into each effect because he wanted one clean success, which of course made his control worse.

Elowen stopped him before the disk could fly.

"Hands down."

Jasper obeyed.

"You're tired," she said.

"I'm fine."

"That sentence has never improved a tired spell."

He looked at the table.

"Go drink water. Five minutes."

Making and Mending was no kinder. Jasper pulled too hard on a repair seam and tore the cloth he was supposed to fix.

Professor Ward handed him a fresh square.

"Slow down."

"I am."

"No. You're trying harder. That is not the same thing."

Jasper hated that she was right.

At lunch he lost two handball games, spilled water across Ava's notes and apologised three times while she dried the paper with a careful thread of Essence.

"Stop saying sorry," Ava said.

"I ruined them."

"You made them wet."

"Same thing."

"They are currently dry."

Harper pointed her fork at him. "Bad nothing."

Jasper frowned. "What?"

"You keep saying nothing is wrong, but this is bad nothing."

Ava looked up from the rescued notes. "Accurate."

Jasper put his fork down.

"I don't know what's wrong."

That was the part he disliked most. He wanted to be good at magic. Not the strongest student and not the strange-water student. Just good. He had been improving for months, and one awful morning had somehow made every improvement feel fake.

Ava thought for a moment. "One bad result does not erase the previous ones."

Harper stared at her. "Can you say it like a person?"

Ava ignored her.

Jasper sighed. "I know."

"You know it with your brain," Ava said. "You don't believe it yet."

Harper leaned across the table. "Remember when I heated the water twelve degrees because I couldn't stop?"

"Yeah."

"Now I can stop at five."

"Usually."

"Rude. The point is I was bad and then less bad. Today you are bad. Tomorrow you can be less bad again."

Jasper smiled despite himself.

"Great support."

"Thank you."
