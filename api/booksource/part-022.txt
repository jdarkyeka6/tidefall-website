
"Everyone does."

"I don't really care."

That answer was so unexpected Jasper laughed. Soren frowned.

"I mean, I care if you're in danger. But I don't know what I'd do with the answer."

He tapped the diagram.

"I can do something with this."

Jasper looked at the page.

"You really like lifts."

"They're excellent."

"Harper was right about the title."

"Both of you are uncultured."

They spent another hour finding photographs of the old system. One showed children from more than a century ago standing beside a wooden platform piled with flour sacks. They looked exactly like Tidefall students and nothing like Tidefall students. Different uniforms. Different hair. Same expressions. One boy in the back was making rabbit ears behind another student's head. Jasper laughed. Soren pointed.

"Proof first-years have always been terrible."

On the walk back to residence, they passed a west window. The Sea was visible beyond it. Jasper felt nothing unusual. He did not check. Soren kept talking about whether a restored lift could run without magic. Jasper listened. That evening Harper asked where he had been.

"Library."

"With Ava?"

"Soren."

Harper looked offended.

"You have other friends?"

Ava did not look up from her book.

"This is healthy."

"I don't like it."

"Also healthy."

Jasper threw a sock at Harper. She caught it and threw it back. Later, when Room Fourteen went quiet, Jasper thought about how much of his year had been shaped by three people standing together. He loved that. But Thorne had been right about one thing. Being needed could become easier than asking other people to carry things. Maybe being a trio could become easier than remembering they were three separate people. Soren's book had contained no Sea mystery. No Deepwater clue. No ancient presence. Jasper had loved the afternoon anyway. That felt important.

CHAPTER SEVENTY-ONE
THE HOOKS

The first hook lasted fourteen minutes. Ava had designed it carefully. Theo had installed it badly. The difference mattered. Their revised observation-ledge equipment system had started as a safety project after the second fall. The old setup was simple. Students brought clipboards, bags and measuring tools onto the ledge. They placed them wherever seemed convenient. Then everyone moved around them. Ava hated this.

"A safety boundary means nothing if the safe zone becomes an obstacle course."

Professor Elowen agreed. So Ava, Theo and the boy who had fallen earlier in the year, whose name was Benji, designed a set of recessed bag hooks and marked equipment bays along the inner wall. No bags near railings. No loose measuring poles across walking routes. Nothing magical. Just hooks. Ava spent three weeks testing spacing. Theo attached the prototype with two screws and confidence. A first-year hung a heavy satchel on it. The hook fell off. Ava stared. Theo stared. The satchel sat on the ground between them. Benji laughed first. That helped. Theo picked up the hook.

"Structural disagreement."

Ava's expression could have damaged stone.

"You used the wrong anchors."

"I used anchors."

"For timber."

"The wall is stone."

"Exactly."

"Right."

"How did you build the window frame?"

"Different project."

"That does not answer the question."

Professor Elowen rescued him by arriving with proper masonry fittings. The second hook lasted. Then the third. By the end of the afternoon, twelve sat evenly along the wall. Painted boxes marked where field equipment should go. Benji tested the route by walking the ledge carrying a clipboard. He stopped where he had fallen months earlier. Jasper watched from farther back. Benji looked over the railing. Not for long. Then stepped away.

"Better," he said.

Ava studied him.

"The hooks?"

"Everything."

She nodded. No one made his fall into a lesson for everyone else while he stood there. No one asked whether the Sea should have saved him. That question had already caused enough damage. Benji hung his bag on the new hook.

"At least I got school furniture out of it."

Theo said, "Legacy." Ava sighed. Jasper smiled. "Legacy" sounded far too grand for a bag hook, which was probably why he liked it. The new system passed inspection. Crowe approved it for every first-year observation ledge. Ava read the approval note three times. Harper found her in Room Fourteen afterward.

"You look happy."

"I am."

"Because of hooks."

"Because something is safer."

Harper sat on her bed.

"You're going to become terrifying when you're older."

Ava considered.

"Good."

The project taught Jasper something too. Not every problem needed ancient knowledge. Some required twelve hooks and the correct masonry anchors. That evening he wrote to his mother. He told her about the new equipment bays. He told her about Soren's lift book. He told her Harper had won the handball final. He did not mention the Sea once. When the letter was finished, he reread it. For months, he had worried that leaving out the Sea meant hiding his life. Now he understood the opposite. His life contained enough other things to fill a page. He sealed the envelope. Outside, the tide moved against the cliff. He let it.

CHAPTER SEVENTY-TWO
THE QUIET FIELD DAY

The last Ocean Studies field day arrived under a sky so blue Harper accused it of showing off. Professor Elowen took the first-years north along the cliff path instead of down toward the harbour. The route stayed well behind the outer edge, winding through low coastal scrub where salt wind flattened the plants into shapes that looked permanently surprised. Every student carried a field book, water bottle and broad hat.

"Australia has discovered sunlight," Theo complained, adjusting his hat.

"Recent development," Harper said.

Ava had applied sunscreen so thoroughly that a white streak remained beside one ear. Jasper did not mention it. He valued his life. The day's task was deliberately ordinary. Measure wind direction. Sketch the coast. Compare rock pools at different heights. Record signs of erosion. No magical sensing. No experimental spells. No special job for Jasper. Elowen had said that directly before they left.

"Today, Mr Holloway is a first-year with a clipboard."

Harper whispered, "Promotion." Jasper elbowed her. He had expected the Sea to make ordinary difficult. Instead it remained completely quiet. Not hidden. Not absent in the way it had been during term break inland. Just not reaching. For the first hour Jasper kept noticing that he did not notice it. That was irritating. By the second hour, he stopped checking. He and Soren measured a shallow pool full of tiny crabs. Harper and Mia argued about whether a gull had stolen Mia's sandwich or whether Mia had placed it "within an established gull economy." Ava found three different lichen types and forgot everyone else existed. Benji tested the new bag-hook procedure when the class stopped at a built observation shelter farther along the path. Nothing went wrong.

That became its own pleasure. At midday they ate on a wide grassed shelf behind a stone wall. The sea filled the horizon. Jasper sat with his back against the warm rock. Harper traded half a cheese sandwich for one of Theo's biscuits. Ava read while eating despite having been told twice not to get crumbs in library books.

"Do you feel it?" Mia asked Jasper.

The question came casually. Not hushed. Jasper looked at the water. He could have searched. He did not.

"Not right now."

Mia nodded.

"Okay."

Then she asked Harper whether the handball trophy was still leaning. That was all. Jasper smiled to himself. At the beginning of the year, any mention of the Sea had turned a conversation into a mystery. Now sometimes it was simply one fact among others. After lunch, Elowen gathered them at a marked viewing point.

"Final exercise," she said. "No instruments for five minutes. Observe."

Harper groaned.

"With our eyes?"

"Traditionally useful."

They spread along the safe inner line. Jasper looked. Not listened. Looked. White foam stitched itself along black rocks below. A fishing boat moved far offshore. Two gulls fought over something too small to identify. Wind bent the grass. Clouds gathered faintly toward the south. The tide had begun to turn. He wrote nothing. Then the pressure came. Soft. Cool. Water. Jasper's first instinct was to focus on it. He stopped himself. The Sea had noticed him. That did not mean it needed anything. He waited. The pressure remained for several breaths. No image. No warning. No direction. Nothing like the storm. Nothing like Harper's fall. Just awareness. Jasper let himself be aware back. Not reaching. Not asking. Then Water moved away. Jasper exhaled. She did not interrupt. The class answered. Wind change. Tide turn. Cloud line. Bird movement.

The strange communication settled into something almost comfortable. Elowen, several metres down the line, had seen his expression. At the end of five minutes she asked everyone what they observed. Jasper said, "A fishing boat." Harper looked at him. He smiled. She understood. No announcement. No special report. Later, while everyone packed up, Elowen came beside him.

"Anything relevant to safety?"

"No."

"Anything you wish to report?"

Jasper considered.

"It was there."

Elowen waited.

"That's it."

"Then that's enough."

They walked back toward Tidefall in the late afternoon. The first-years became tired and loud in waves. Harper started a terrible song. Theo improved it by making it worse. Ava threatened to walk behind the group. Jasper joined the chorus purely to annoy her. Halfway home, he realized he had learned something on the quietest Sea day of the year. Silence was not rejection. Attention was not instruction. And communication did not have to become a problem before it counted. Sometimes the Sea was there. Sometimes Jasper knew. Then both of them continued with their day.

CHAPTER SEVENTY-THREE
LETTERS ABOUT ORDERS

Jasper's mother hated the idea of Choosing Tide for exactly seven minutes. Then she hated the name.

"You are telling me the school puts seven-year-olds in moving seawater to decide where they live next year."

Jasper held the mirror token closer. Her face filled the little silver surface.

"Shallow moving seawater," he said.

His father appeared behind her.

"Loads."

"Rails?"

"Not really. Platforms."

His mother closed her eyes.

"Of course there aren't rails."

Jasper smiled. The academy had scheduled family calls before Choosing Tide so parents could ask questions. "Yeah."

"Yeah."

"No."

Three families. Three sets of people who had trusted Tidefall with children barely old enough to reach some of the shelves. The thought made the ceremony feel larger. Jasper had mostly imagined the three of them in the basin. He had forgotten the people watching.

Home and Tidefall folded together. Jasper had spent the year learning that boarding school did not replace one with the other. He belonged to both. Choosing Tide would not replace that either. At least he hoped not.

CHAPTER SEVENTY-FOUR
HOT WEATHER

Tidefall in early summer smelled different. Winter made the academy smell like wet wool, old stone and soup. Summer brought salt, sunscreen, hot timber and the faint sharp scent of eucalyptus from inland wind. By ten in the morning, every first-year had been reminded twice to fill a water bottle. By eleven, Harper had lost hers.

"It was here."

Ava looked around the courtyard.

"Where?"

"Somewhere."

"Outstanding."

Jasper pointed to the wall. Harper's pink-marked bottle sat in full view beside the handball square.

"Found it."

"You did not find it."

"I identified its position."

"That is finding."

The last fortnight of year felt unlike the rest. Formal lessons shortened. Assessment was mostly finished. Teachers moved practical work into mornings and used the hottest part of afternoons for indoor projects. Windows stood open. Uniform rules loosened around jumpers and jackets. The dining hall replaced heavy puddings with fruit, cold custard and ice blocks that disappeared faster than any magical object Jasper had seen. One Thursday the entire first-year cohort went to Tidefall's sheltered beach. Not the cliffs. Not the lower harbour. A crescent of sand east of the academy protected by rock arms and supervised by enough staff to make Harper complain there were "more adults than ocean." Swimming was optional. Jasper chose ankle-deep water. Harper chose everywhere. Nobody believed him. Jasper ended up batting. He was terrible. Then at Jasper.

Ava chose a towel in the shade for the first twenty minutes, then admitted the water looked good and went in up to her knees. Mia organized beach cricket with a piece of driftwood. Theo claimed driftwood was traditional equipment. Soren bowled a soft rubber ball that Jasper somehow sent directly backward. It landed beside Professor Ashcroft's chair. Ashcroft looked at the ball.

"An innovative direction."

"Sorry."

"I did not say effective."

Harper took over and immediately hit the ball into shallow water. Theo ran after it. A small wave pushed the ball back before he reached it. Everyone stopped. Jasper felt Water. Barely. A cool flicker. Harper looked at him.

"Did it do that?"

Jasper looked at the ball rolling in foam.

"Could've been a wave."

Ava smiled.

"Correct answer."

Theo picked it up.

"Thank you, suspiciously convenient wave."

The game continued. That mattered. Months ago, a strange wave would have ended the afternoon. Now uncertainty could exist without swallowing everything around it. After cricket, Harper convinced Jasper to go deeper.

"No."

"Already there."

"Slightly more knees."

"That is not anatomy."

She walked backward through the water.

"Come on."
