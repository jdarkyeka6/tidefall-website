Harper thought.

"Maybe."

The next day she asked Professor Elowen. Elowen said yes without making it a big thing. Harper stopped before crossing. The repairs were obvious. Everything looked safer. Professor Elowen waited. Harper took one step. Then another. Stopped. The sea rolled below. No strange swell.

Saturday morning, the four of them walked to the north ledge. Professor Elowen unlocked the final gate. The old stone barrier had been raised and reinforced with vertical metal rails. The drainage groove had been filled and resurfaced. The painted student line sat farther back. It also looked exactly like the place Harper had fallen. She stood under the arch. CHAPTER THIRTY-ONE
THE QUESTION AVA COULD NOT MEASURE

Ava trusted numbers because numbers behaved.

They did not always give people the answer they wanted, but once written down they usually stayed where they were put.

The Water Titan did not.

That became Ava's problem.

For months she had collected observations.

Dates.

Weather.

Tide height.

Locations.

Jasper's descriptions of pressure and images.

She had expected a pattern eventually.

Instead, the notebook became messier the more accurate she made it.

The presence appeared on calm days and stormy ones.

Near docks and high galleries.

Sometimes before Jasper noticed anything unusual in the water.

Sometimes afterward.

It had moved for Harper after ignoring smaller slips.

It had shown Jasper impressions that might have been memories, but never when asked.

One wet Thursday, Ava shut the notebook harder than necessary.

Harper looked up from the floor.

"What did paper do?"

"Nothing."

"Then why are you attacking it?"

"There is no pattern."

Jasper sat on his bed trying to untangle a practice knot.

"Maybe that is the pattern."

Ava stared at him.

He immediately regretted speaking.

"Explain."

Jasper tried.

"If it can choose things, then it doesn't have to happen on a timetable."

Ava looked offended by the concept.

Harper laughed.

"Ava has discovered people are bad data."

"People have patterns."

"They can also change their minds."

Ava looked down at the notebook.

That was exactly the problem.

Choice made prediction unreliable.

"Then how do we know anything?"

Jasper shrugged. "We know what already happened."

"That is not enough."

"Why?"

Ava opened her mouth.

Then closed it.

Jasper had never seen Ava lose a question before.

That afternoon, Foundations made the problem worse in a useful way.

Pairs had to move three objects across a table without touching them. One object had a training enchantment that changed its weight at unpredictable moments.

Ava hated it immediately.

Her first plan failed when a wooden cube became heavier halfway through the lift.

She reset.

The second plan failed when it became lighter instead.

On the third attempt, Ava stopped deciding the exact route before she began.

She left space to adjust.

The cube changed weight.

Ava lowered it.

Changed the amount of Essence she was using.

Lifted again.

Finished.

Professor Elowen nodded.

"Better."

Ava looked at the objects. "I did not predict it."

"You adapted."

"That feels worse."

"It is often more useful."

Back in Room Fourteen, Ava opened the Map of Things That Should Not Happen.

She created a new page.

KNOWN FACTS.

Not theories.

Not predictions.

Only things they had enough evidence to say.

Jasper experiences a repeatable sensation associated with unusual sea events.

Multiple people have witnessed physically unusual water movement.

Harper's fall involved a swell that did not match ordinary conditions.

Jasper did not cause that swell.

Some impressions Jasper receives contain information or viewpoints he did not consciously possess.

Historical Tidefall records describe similar experiences.

Then Ava made a second heading.

UNKNOWN.

What the Water Titan is.

Why Jasper senses it.

Whether it wants anything.

Whether it understands him.

Why it acted for Harper.

Whether Jasper understands it at all.

Harper looked over her shoulder.

"That's a lot of unknown."

"Yes."

"You okay?"

Ava thought.

"Annoyed."

"Your normal okay."

Ava almost smiled.

Jasper pointed at the list. "Can I add one?"

"What?"

"Whether it likes handball."

Ava stared at him.

Harper burst out laughing.

Ava wrote it.

Jasper looked shocked. "You actually put that down."

"Unknown is unknown."

The notebook felt less frightening after that.

It no longer had to solve the mystery.

It only had to be honest about what they knew.

CHAPTER THIRTY-TWO
THE SECOND FALL

The second fall happened four weeks after Harper's. The weather was calm. That mattered. No storm. No strange pressure before the lesson. No reason for anyone to expect anything. Ocean Studies had returned to the main observation terrace while Professor Elowen rotated groups through coastline measurement. The class knew the safety rules better now. Harper especially. She stayed well behind the line. Jasper was measuring wind direction with Ava when someone shouted. A smaller first-year boy from North residence had been carrying a chart board toward the inner rail. Another student's bag lay on the ground behind him. He stepped backward onto the strap. His foot tangled. He fell. Not over the cliff immediately. The chart board dropped. Professor Elowen moved. The boy's fingers slipped. Jasper's heart stopped. His chest remained empty. No pressure. Not to command. To find. Nothing. No swell rose. No curved wave. No impossible lift. The sea remained sea. Jasper heard Harper gasp.

His hands caught the stone edge of the inner step. He slid down a short slope beyond the student line and struck a narrow rock protrusion that slowed him. Then he went over the final lip. He reached inward by instinct. The boy fell the remaining distance into the water. He hit the surface hard and disappeared. Professor Elowen was already shouting.

"Rescue station! Rope!"

An older student on the lower dock responded immediately. A ring was thrown. The boy surfaced coughing and flailing. A current pulled him toward the rocks. The older student clipped into a safety line and descended partway to reach him. Seconds stretched. Jasper stood frozen. Still nothing. No presence. No choice he could feel. The boy caught the ring. Staff hauled him toward the platform. He was dragged safely onto stone. Alive. Shaken. Bruised and scraped. Nothing worse. The class was moved indoors. Nobody spoke. Harper sat beside Jasper. Her face looked exactly as it had in the infirmary window reflection after her own fall.

"It didn't move."

Jasper shook his head. Ava sat opposite them. She had not opened her notebook.

"It chose."

The words landed differently now. Jasper looked at her.

"You don't know that."

"No."

Ava swallowed.

"But if Harper's wave was intentional, then not acting is also a choice."

Harper's jaw tightened.

"Why?"

Nobody had an answer. The boy was taken to the infirmary. He went home for several days with his family. The accident investigation found no major structural failure. A loose bag strap. A bad step. One of those horrible chains of small things. The academy added another rule about keeping walkways clear during practical lessons. Some students whispered that the sea liked Harper better. Harper heard. She nearly started a fight. Ava caught her arm.

"Not worth it."

"They said—"

"I heard."

"It's not funny."

"I know."

Harper stopped. Jasper walked away from the corridor before anyone asked him why the water had not moved. He ended up in the library. Not the tide section. The inland corner. He sat between two shelves and stared at nothing. After a while, Soren found him. Soren did not ask questions. He sat nearby and read. Ten minutes later, Harper and Ava arrived. Harper sat on Jasper's left. Ava on his right. Soren looked at all three. Then moved to another aisle without comment. Jasper whispered, "I waited for it." Harper said, "I know."

"I thought it would catch him."

Ava said, "So did I."

"Why didn't it?"

Silence. Harper picked at the edge of her sleeve.

"Maybe it couldn't."

Ava said, "Maybe."

"Maybe it wasn't there."

"Maybe."

"Maybe I was different because Jasper was there."

Jasper's stomach twisted.

"I was there today."

"I know."

Ava looked at both of them.

"We are doing the thing."

"What thing?" Harper asked.

"Trying to turn unknown into an answer because not knowing feels awful."

Harper looked down. Jasper leaned his head against the shelf.

"It does feel awful."

"Yes."

That was all Ava had. For once, enough. That night Jasper did not sleep for a long time. He kept hearing the splash. Kept feeling the emptiness where presence should have been. When he finally drifted off, he dreamed of dark water. There was no shape below him. Only distance. Jasper called into it. Nothing answered. He woke before sunrise. The sea beyond the western window rolled normally. He did not ask it why.

CHAPTER THIRTY-THREE
THE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN SAVE AND CHOOSE

The academy held no Ocean Studies for a week. Not because the sea was dangerous. Because two first-year falls in one winter was enough for every coastal lesson procedure to be reviewed from the beginning. Professor Crowe led the review personally. Students saw measuring teams on terraces. New floor markings appeared. Loose equipment rules changed. Clip points were added to certain older observation areas. Harper approved.

"Finally."

Ava looked at her.

"You complained about every safety rule in first term."

"Old me was stupid."

"You are four months older."

"Exactly. Wisdom."

The North residence boy returned on Monday. He had a bruised shoulder and a bandage on one hand. Everyone tried not to stare. That was worse than staring. At lunch, Harper walked over to him. Jasper and Ava watched from their table. Harper said something. The boy looked surprised. Then smiled. Harper came back.

"What did you say?" Jasper asked.

"Nothing."

Ava raised an eyebrow. Harper sighed.

"I said people are going to be weird for a few days and then Theo will break something."

Theo, two seats away, looked up.

"Why me?"

"History."

The boy's return helped. He was alive. He attended class. He complained about missing handball. The second fall became an accident rather than a ghost story. The Sea question remained. Thorne called Jasper, Harper and Ava to his study again. Jasper was beginning to dislike the furniture there.

"You have all had time to think," Thorne said.

Harper replied, "Too much."

"What do you believe happened?"

Ava answered first.

"We know Harper's fall involved abnormal water movement. We know the second fall did not. We don't know why."

Thorne nodded. Harper said, "I hate the word saved." Thorne looked at her.

"Why?"

"Because people say the sea saved me like it owns the whole story. Elowen saved me. The lower platform mattered. Jasper saw me. I got lucky with where I hit. The wave changed it. It wasn't everything."

"Fair."

Harper continued.

"And if it saved me, then it didn't save him. That sounds like it wanted him hurt."

Jasper looked at her. He had thought the same thing without saying it. Thorne folded his hands.

"Choosing not to intervene is not always the same as wanting harm."

Ava leaned forward.

"But if it could have intervened?"

"We don't know that it could."

"If it could."

Thorne considered.

"Then we still would not know why it did not."

Harper groaned.

"More unknown."

"Yes."

Jasper said, "I felt nothing."

"That matters," Thorne replied.

"Maybe it wasn't there."

"Possible."

"Then the difference isn't who it chose. It's whether it was present."

"Possible."

Ava's eyes sharpened.

"Selection requires opportunity."

Harper looked at her.

"English."

"You cannot say it rejected him if it wasn't there."

Harper breathed out. That helped. Jasper asked, "But what if it was there and I couldn't feel it?" Thorne's expression remained frustratingly calm.

"Then we have another unknown."

Jasper slumped. Thorne almost smiled.

"You are trying to build morality out of two incidents. Be careful."

"Morality?"

"Good sea. Bad sea. Protective sea. Cruel sea. Those are human categories. You do not yet know what kind of mind, if any, you are encountering."

Harper said, "It chose something."

"I agree."

The room went quiet. That was the first time Thorne had said it directly. I agree. Not possibly. Not records.

"You think it chose to move for me," Harper said.

"Yes."

She looked down.

"Why?"

"I don't know."

Harper nodded. This time the answer did not make her angry. After the meeting, the trio walked to Making and Mending. Professor Ward had them repair torn practice bags. Simple work. Jasper liked it. Harper threaded a cord through a ripped seam by hand before reinforcing it magically. Ava tested each repair by pulling harder than necessary. Nothing about the class involved oceans. At the end, Ward held up one patched bag.

"A repair does not make something new. It lets the existing thing continue."

Jasper thought about Harper. The wave had not made her invincible. It had changed one moment enough for everything else to continue. That was different from the word save. He liked changed better.

CHAPTER THIRTY-FOUR
MIDYEAR

Midyear break began under rain. The harbour boat left Tidefall through grey mist and rough water. Jasper sat inside this time. He had learned that open-deck bravery in winter mostly meant being cold for no reason. Harper called him weak while wearing two jumpers. Ava had packed snacks according to travel duration. Harper ate one thirty minutes early and ruined the system.

"Now the distribution is wrong."

"The food still exists."

"That is not the point."

Jasper laughed. As the boat moved away from Tidefall, the presence faded. He felt the exact moment. Not a door closing. Distance. Like a radio signal becoming weaker until it could no longer be distinguished from noise. Jasper told Ava. She noted the location on the boat map. Harper watched.

"I thought we weren't making everything data."

Ava said, "Distance is useful." Jasper agreed. Not summer warm. By the time the ordinary coast appeared, the presence was gone. Home was warmer than Tidefall. Perth winter sunlight still filled the kitchen in the middle of the day, and Jasper's father complained dramatically about mornings below ten degrees. His mum hugged him for a long time. Then held him at arm's length.

"You look tired."

"I'm fine."

She raised an eyebrow. Jasper corrected.

"A bit tired."

"Better."

He told his parents about Harper's fall in person even though letters had already covered it. The telling changed when their faces were in front of him. His mum went pale. His dad stopped making jokes. Jasper explained the second fall too.

"And the sea did nothing?" his father asked.

"Nothing weird. Staff got him."

His mother stared at the table.

"Do you still want to go back?"

The same question from parent weekend. Jasper answered the same way.

"Yes."

This time she nodded without asking twice. The break was quieter than the first. Jasper did not spend every day missing school. He saw family. Played games. Went to the beach once and felt ordinary water again. He liked ordinary water. He also dreamed twice. The first dream was nothing but dark waves seen from far above. The second was stranger. Jasper stood on a beach at night. The tide kept arriving but never retreated. Each wave stopped at his feet, held for a moment, then disappeared rather than flowing back. Far offshore, something waited. Jasper did not ask what it was. He simply stood. The dream ended. He wrote it down the next morning. Not because Ava asked.

Because he wanted to remember without deciding what it meant. That felt like progress too. Harper sent a letter midway through break. I WENT SWIMMING. Then read on. Jasper stared at the first line. POOL. NOT OCEAN. I'M NOT INSANE. Choosing Tide waited too. Four Orders. Permanent placement. Brannor. Velmor. Calyx. Nocten. The Tide would.

MUM MADE ME DO TWO LESSONS BECAUSE I KEPT SAYING I COULD SWIM FINE. IT WAS ACTUALLY OKAY. DO NOT TELL ANYONE I SAID THAT. Ava's letter arrived the same day. It contained a small table titled COASTAL INCIDENT FACTS WE ACTUALLY KNOW. Jasper laughed for nearly a minute. He wrote back to both. The second half of first year waited. They had begun hearing more about it from older students. First-years would not choose. At home, that sounded like another school tradition. Jasper did not yet understand how complicated the word Tide was about to become.
