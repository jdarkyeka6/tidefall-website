Jasper's chest tightened. Good question. Elowen answered.

"Policy, for one. Ethics, for another. Jasper's reports can inform decisions. He is not emergency infrastructure."

"Is that written?"

Elowen smiled slightly.

"It will be."

His mum nodded.

"Good."

Jasper stared at her.

"You just made a school rule."

"Excellent."

His father said, "Your mother has always wanted administrative power."

"Correct."

The call became easier after that. They talked about exams. Choosing Tide. Whether Jasper had enough clean socks. His mum asked which Order he wanted.

"Don't know."

"Can you choose?"

"No."

"I dislike that already."

Jasper laughed.

"Harper agrees."

The mirror dimmed after forty minutes. Jasper sat in the quiet room afterward. Professor Elowen packed away the activation card.

"Your mother is right."

"About what?"

"We need explicit policy."

"Because of me?"

"Because institutions should not rely on goodwill when power differences exist."

Jasper stared.

"What does that mean?"

Elowen simplified.

"It shouldn't depend on whether the current Headmaster is nice. Rules should protect you from being treated like equipment."

"Oh."

That made sense. By the following week, Tidefall had drafted an internal guideline for unusual student magical sensitivities. Consent where possible. No deliberate provocation for testing without formal safety review. No requirement that a student reproduce an experience for staff curiosity. Emergency reports treated as information, not commands. Jasper was not named. He liked that even more. Something strange about him had improved the school without turning him into the school. His mother approved. Her next letter began: BETTER. Then asked about socks.

CHAPTER FIFTY-FIVE
THE SAFETY PROJECT

Professor Ward gave first-years the worst possible assignment. A group presentation. Harper considered this worse than exams.

"I can do things. I don't want to stand there explaining things I did."

Ava looked delighted.

"We have to create a safety improvement proposal."

"Exactly. Talking."

"With evidence."

"Worse."

Groups were assigned randomly. Jasper, Harper and Ava were not together. Jasper worked with Mia, Soren and Leila. Harper got Theo, two quiet students from East residence and a boy who had not spoken more than ten words in any class all year. Harper raised her hand.

"No."

"Risk: sadness."

"No."

Jasper's group chose library ladders. Not exciting. Exactly why Ward approved. Students climbed rolling ladders to reach upper shelves. The ladders locked automatically when weight was applied, but the lock indicator was a tiny brass pin easy to miss. Mia proposed a light that turned green when locked. Soren proposed simply moving all books lower. Leila said the library contained tens of thousands of books and asked where. Soren conceded. Of course.

Jasper suggested a simple ward tone if weight was applied before locking. Their final proposal combined both. Harper's group chose handball courts. Their risk was balls crossing walking paths during crowded lunch periods. Theo suggested a giant wall. Harper said giant walls were ugly.

They eventually proposed low magical boundary strips that softened balls crossing court edges without affecting play inside. Ava's group chose coastal class equipment. Everyone expected this after the falls. Instead Ava rejected cliff barriers.

"Adults are already reviewing them. Our project should solve something not currently solved."

The North residence boy suggested bag hooks. His fall had begun because he stepped onto a loose strap. They designed fold-flat hooks and marked equipment zones behind student working areas. Simple. Cheap. Useful. When presentation day arrived, Jasper's group went second. Jasper hated standing in front of the room. His mouth went dry. Mia started confidently. Soren explained the current ladder lock. Leila demonstrated the proposed light. Jasper explained the warning tone. He stumbled over one sentence. Nobody cared. They finished. Harper's group was chaos until the moment they began. Then Harper became strangely good at presenting because she spoke the same way she argued.

"Balls go here. People go here. When ball becomes people problem, line slows ball. Easy."

Professor Ward said, "Clear." Ava's group presented last. The room became quiet. Harper sat down looking shocked by praise. The North residence boy explained the bag strap accident himself. He did not dramatise it.

Then Ava showed the fold-flat hook design. Ward approved a trial installation in two Ocean Studies areas. The boy smiled. After class Harper said, "That's cool." He nodded.

"Better than everyone staring at me."

Harper understood.

"Yeah."

The project changed how Jasper thought about accidents. Rules after Deepwater. New barriers after Harper's fall. Equipment zones after the second fall. Storm gate repairs. Not every consequence had to become fear. Sometimes it became a hook on a wall that prevented someone else from stepping on a bag. The academy was not safe because nothing bad happened. It became safer by remembering what did. Ava wrote that sentence in her notebook. Harper accused her of becoming philosophical. Ava blamed Jasper. Jasper refused responsibility.

Small wooden figure.

Jasper smiled.

Jasper pinned his note above his bed. Not because it proved he was special. Because months earlier he could barely move a cloth. That progress belonged entirely to him. No Sea involved. He liked that more than he could explain.

CHAPTER FIFTY-SEVEN
THE REOPENING

The lower harbour reopened fully in early December with what Professor Crowe insisted was not a ceremony. Harper pointed at the ribbon stretched across the access point.

"That is a ceremony ribbon."

"Temporary safety marker."

"Ribbon."

Thorne cut it anyway. Students laughed. The work barge had been patched and repainted. New gate guides replaced cracked fittings. Observation rails were stronger. Jasper's group's equipment hooks had been installed along the wall as a trial. Benji saw them and smiled. That mattered more than Jasper expected. The harbour was not restored to the exact version that existed before the storm. It had changed because people had learned things. During the reopening tour, Jasper felt the Water Titan briefly near the channel. The pressure was calm. No images. No urgency. He told Thorne anyway.

"Thank you," the Headmaster said.

Nothing more was required. On the way back, Harper asked whether they could use the harbour independently next year. Ava immediately listed the training requirements. Harper regretted asking. Jasper looked up at the Academy rising above them.

There were whole versions of Tidefall ahead that he had not seen yet: older classes, Order life, new parts of the grounds, things first-years were not allowed to do. The mystery that had once filled almost every corner of his attention became smaller in the best possible way.

Thorne cut it anyway. Students laughed. The work barge had returned, patched and repainted. New gate guides replaced the cracked ring system. The inner platform rails were stronger. Ava's equipment-zone hooks from the safety project had been installed as a trial along the observation wall. The North residence boy saw them and smiled. That mattered more than the ribbon. The whole first-year group visited during green-flag conditions. They practised rescue lines one last time before term ended. Jasper landed the ring on target first throw. Harper missed. She stared at him.

Ava sighed. They threw again. Harper hit. Jasper missed. Balance restored. After the class, Professor Elowen allowed the trio to remain on the upper dock platform for ten minutes while staff packed equipment. The sea was calm. Summer light turned the water almost green inside the harbour. Jasper felt the Titan-presence faintly. Harper sat on a bench. Ava leaned against the rail inside the safe line.

They sat quietly. A maintenance boat entered the harbour. The gate remained open. Water passed around the hull. Ordinary. Then the presence brushed Jasper's awareness. An impression came. Not warning. Not memory. The repaired gate from below. Open channel. Current moving cleanly. Then a small warm point on the platform. Jasper. The impression vanished. He smiled. Ava noticed. It was still important. It was not the only important thing.

CHAPTER FIFTY-EIGHT
THE BEACH DAY

The first-years finished exams on a Wednesday. By Thursday morning they had become impossible. There were still lessons, technically, but the serious part of the year had loosened. Teachers stopped assigning new written work. Foundations became games designed to test skills they already knew. Making and Mending turned into practical projects. Ocean Studies moved outside whenever weather allowed. At breakfast, Professor Elowen announced a supervised beach day for the following Monday. The first-year section erupted. Harper did not. Jasper noticed before she had time to hide it. The beach was not the tide-pool shelf where she had slipped harmlessly months earlier. This was a sandy cove south of Tidefall used for supervised swimming by older students and staff. First-years would have a shallow-water safety session, picnic and coastal games. Ava glanced at Jasper.
