ent sign instead.

Harper helped a younger student free a bag strap from a chair.

Jasper left his own things where they were.

Nobody ran toward danger. Nobody needed him to sense anything.

They simply followed the plan.

Inside Assembly Hall Two, residence groups counted students. Theo arrived late from the toilets with a prefect, looking deeply offended that an emergency drill had included his bladder.

Professor Crowe addressed them when the count was complete.

"Two issues. Several students went back for belongings. Do not. One group used the route they remembered instead of today's marked route. Emergency plans are not memory tests. Follow current information."

Ava looked as though Crowe had personally spoken to her.

On the walk back to lunch, Jasper said, "You didn't use your map."

"It was old."

"Growth."

Ava glared at him.

Harper added, "Record that."

Ava did not.

The food had gone cold.

Harper declared this the only genuine emergency of the day.

By dessert, even Jasper was laughing.

The drill had mattered without becoming a disaster, which seemed increasingly important to him. Safety was not heroics. Most of the time it was noticing the sign, following the teacher and leaving your lunch behind.

Jasper liked that kind of responsibility better.

CHAPTER NINETY-FIVE
THE THREE CHAIRS

The first-year common room emptied faster than Jasper expected.

Furniture remained.

People disappeared into packing, family visits and final activities.

One afternoon, only three chairs near the western fireplace were occupied.

Jasper.

Harper.

Ava.

There was no ceremonial reason for it.

They had simply ended up there.

Harper held the heat-changing mug she had bought in Lowtide.

While the hot chocolate was warm, the mug read:

THIS WAS A LIE.

As it cooled, the words changed back to:

I AM CALM.

Jasper laughed every time.

Ava claimed she did not find it funny.

Nobody believed her.

They talked about summer.

Harper planned beach days, a family trip and learning how to make the bracelet knot properly.

Ava planned to read.

Then she stopped herself.

"I intend to read."

Harper stared. "That is the same plan wearing glasses."

"It is less quantified."

Jasper planned card tricks, although Thorne had not suggested them yet.

For a while, the conversation stayed easy.

Then Harper asked, "What if we come back different?"

Ava answered immediately. "We will."

Harper frowned. "I mean bad different."

Jasper looked at the fire.

"Then we work it out."

"Very simple."

"Probably not simple."

Ava nodded. "Still the answer."

Harper's mug cooled.

I AM CALM.

She reheated it with too much Essence.

The message changed instantly.

THIS WAS A LIE.

Ava said, "You have learned nothing."

Harper tested the temperature with one finger.

"I stopped before boiling it."

Ava considered.

"Fine. Limited growth."

They talked about Orders next.

Not predictions.

Not probabilities.

Just the fact that in a few days the three of them might be wearing different colours.

Harper hated the thought.

Ava did too, though she expressed this by explaining that Order placement did not change existing residence friendships.

Jasper said, "We can still meet."

"Obviously," Harper said.

"Then why are you worried?"

"Because obviously and feeling are different."

Ava looked at her.

Harper looked suspicious.

"What?"

"That was intelligent."

"Rude."

Jasper smiled.

The fire clicked softly.

Outside, somebody shouted over a handball game.

Nothing ancient happened.

No Titan appeared.

No teacher gave them a lesson.

Three chairs remained three chairs.

Jasper thought he would remember them anyway.

CHAPTER NINETY-SIX
THE REHEARSAL WITHOUT WATER

Choosing Tide rehearsal contained no tide.

That was deliberate.

The first-years walked through the empty basin while staff explained where to enter, where families would sit, what each bell meant and where students should wait after reaching an Order platform.

Harper considered the whole thing boring.

She also asked more questions than anybody else.

Professor Crowe noticed.

"Rehearsal is useful?"

"No."

"Yet you have asked about every step."

"I like being prepared to ignore instructions correctly."

Ava closed her eyes.

The basin looked different without water.

Carved channels crossed the floor from the central marker toward four Order platforms. A brass emergency line ran beneath one edge. Staff demonstrated how pulling it would close the Academy-controlled sea gate and stop the ceremony.

Harper raised her hand. "So you can stop the Tide."

"The ceremony," Crowe corrected. "The ocean does not belong to our switch."

Jasper liked that distinction.

Students practised entering one at a time.

Walk to the centre.

Stop.

Wait for the signal.

Follow the marked exit after placement.

Do not run.

Harper ran.

She was made to repeat the route.

"Targeted," she muttered.

Ava said, "Entirely self-created."

The rehearsal also showed them the support rooms.

Healers had towels and warming blankets ready.

Staff had spare grey uniform pieces.

Order edging had been sorted in labelled trays.

Ava stared at the trays.

"Do they know how many of each they need?"

"No," the staff member said.

"So what if the numbers are uneven?"

"We keep extras."

Ava seemed deeply relieved by inventory.

Families would arrive before breakfast on Choosing morning and sit in the gallery.

Students would eat first.

Then change into grey ceremonial uniform.

No Order colours until after placement.

Jasper touched the plain scarf folded over his arm.

For most of the year, Orders had been something older students wore.

Soon the grey would be temporary.

At the centre of the dry basin, Jasper looked toward Brannor's platform.

Then Velmor.

Calyx.

Nocten.

He felt nothing from the Water Titan.

Good.

Professor Crowe called the next student's name.

Jasper stepped aside.

The rehearsal continued.

By the end, Harper had stopped calling it pointless.

She did not admit this.

Nobody asked.

CHAPTER NINETY-SEVEN
THE AWARDS THAT DID NOT MATTER

Theo invented awards during lunch.

This was immediately dangerous.

He cut circles from old card and wrote titles across them in large black marker.

MOST LIKELY TO KNOW THE HOMEWORK: AVA.

MOST LIKELY TO TURN ANYTHING INTO A RACE: HARPER.

MOST LIKELY TO STARE AT WATER: JASPER.

Jasper accepted his card.

"Lazy."

"Accurate," Theo replied.

Soren received MOST LIKELY TO READ A MANUAL VOLUNTARILY.

Mia got MOST LIKELY TO BEAT HARPER WHEN IT MATTERS.

Harper objected for several minutes.

Benji received BEST SCHOOL FURNITURE LEGACY for the safety hooks and laughed hard enough to snort.

Then everybody started making awards.

Theo received MOST LIKELY TO CREATE A RULE NAMED AFTER HIM.

Harper received BEST RECOVERY FROM AN ARGUMENT SHE WAS DEFINITELY LOSING.

"That is not an award."

"It is now."

Ava made one for Harper.

MOST IMPROVED RELATIONSHIP WITH THE WORD NO.

Harper stared at it.

"Mean."

"Praise."

Jasper made Ava one.

MOST LIKELY TO ASK BEFORE WRITING IT DOWN NOW.

Ava went quiet.

Then smiled.

She made Jasper's carefully.

MOST LIKELY TO SAY I DON'T KNOW CORRECTLY.

Harper groaned.

"Your awards are emotional."

Jasper handed her another card.

BEST DRUMMER WHO KNOWS ONE RHYTHM.

"Better."

The awards had no authority.

Nobody won anything.

No teacher recorded them.

Most were thrown away by the end of lunch.

A few went into pockets.

Jasper put his two in the history box.

Later he found Harper had added another one without telling him.

MOST IMPROVED AT NOT BEING RESPONSIBLE FOR THE ENTIRE OCEAN.

He stared at it.

Then laughed.

Some school awards mattered because adults decided they did.

These mattered because they did not.

CHAPTER NINETY-EIGHT
THE ONE HUNDRED STEPS

There were not one hundred steps between West Residence and Founders' Courtyard.

There were ninety-four.

Harper had been calling them the Hundred Steps all year.

Ava finally corrected her during the last week.

"Impossible."

"Count."

They counted.

Ninety-four.

Harper accused Tidefall of removing six steps.

Jasper said, "Maybe you skipped numbers."

"I don't skip."

Ava stared at her.

"You skip physical steps whenever possible."

"Different."

They climbed again.

Ninety-four.

Harper looked genuinely upset.

"The Hundred Steps is a better name."

"It is not the name," Ava said.

"I named them."

"Incorrectly."

At the top, Harper declared that truth should occasionally serve branding.

Ava said this was how misinformation began.

The argument lasted until lunch.

That evening, Jasper climbed the steps alone to collect a book he had left in the common room.

He counted without meaning to.

Ninety-four.

Halfway down, he realised how many ordinary places had become part of the year.

Not the ledge.

Not the harbour.

Not the Choosing Basin.

A staircase.

A badly named staircase.

He had run up it late for class.

Carried laundry down it.

Listened to Harper complain on it.

Stopped while Ava retied a shoe.

Nothing important had happened there.

That was probably why it mattered.

Back in Room Fourteen, Jasper dropped a sock behind his trunk while packing.

The room lamp did not reach.

He lifted one hand.

"Blinka."

A small warm light appeared above his fingers.

No dramatic effort.

No teacher watching.

No one applauded.

Jasper found the sock, cancelled the light and kept packing.

His first spell at Tidefall had taken several attempts to move a piece of cloth less than a centimetre.

His last ordinary spell of first year was useful enough that he barely thought about it.

Jasper liked that more than any showcase.

From the corridor, Harper shouted, "Ninety-four is still wrong."

Ava shouted back, "Arithmetic does not care."

Jasper laughed.

Some things had not changed at all.

CHAPTER NINETY-NINE
THE RETURN CART

Library return carts appeared in the halls before summer.

Students had to return ordinary term loans even if they planned to borrow books again for the holidays.

Ava returned everything on the first day.

Jasper found one overdue book beneath the history box.

Harper had five.

"How?"

"Books accumulate."

"They have dates."

"Dates accumulate."

They carried Harper's stack to the library.

Imani Grey scanned each volume.

"Two days late. Three on time."

Harper looked at Ava. "Better than expected."

"The expectation was floor-level."

One book had a bent cover.

Harper admitted she had used it beneath a wobbling chair leg.

Grey closed her eyes.

"Books are not furniture repair."

"It worked."

"You owe the repair desk fifteen minutes."

Harper accepted the sentence.

Jasper helped because Harper and adhesive film were a dangerous combination.

Under supervision, they repaired the cover until the book looked almost new.

Harper ran one finger over it.

"I fixed consequences."

Grey said, "You participated in repair after causing damage."

"Same."

"Not exactly."

By the time they left the library, Jasper had begun noticing how much of the final week involved giving things back.

Books.

Room keys.

Borrowed sports equipment.

Practice materials.

Spaces that had felt like theirs because they used them every day.

Back in West Residence, transport staff had begun moving trunks into the corridor.

Jasper found one with his room number and started wheeling it toward the lift.

Ava stopped him.

"That is not yours."

He looked at the label.

JASPER HOLLAND.

Jasper stared.

Harper laughed so hard she had to sit down.

"Close."

"There is another Jasper?"

"Apparently."

They found Jasper Holloway's trunk two doors farther along.

The mistake became funnier because the wrong Jasper turned out to be a Year Three student from East Residence who was equally confused to find Jasper's smaller trunk waiting for him.

Staff corrected the tags.

Nothing magical had caused the problem.

Nobody blamed folded space.

Two people had similar names.

That felt almost refreshing.

Ava checked it once.

Harper said, "You have both become me."

"No," Ava replied.

Jasper closed the lid.

Most things were packed.

The history box was not.

That would stay out until the end.

CHAPTER ONE HUNDRED
ROOM FOURTEEN'S LAST NIGHT

The last night in Room Fourteen began with three children refusing to sleep. This was not unusual. The reasons were. Tomorrow was Choosing Tide. After that, the room would still exist for two more nights until departure. But it would no longer be theirs in the same way. Placement would be known. Next year's residence would have names. The question hanging over every conversation would finally have an answer. Harper lay on the Bag Bed because her own was already stripped.

"I don't like that this is empty."

Jasper looked around. The walls had pale squares where their drawings had been. Shelves were bare. Trunks closed. Ava had left one pencil on her desk because, she said, "packing every writing instrument before the final morning is absurd." Harper had packed none until Ava threatened to do it for her. Jasper sat on the floor under the western window. The Sea sounded normal outside.

"We could just stay here next year," Harper said.

Ava looked at her.

"No."

"Why?"

"Because this is first-year residence."

"Rules can change."

"Not because you don't want to move a trunk."

"That is a valid policy reason."

Jasper smiled. The room had started as four beds and strangers. Then three beds and one empty one. Then the empty one became storage. Then history. Tonight Harper lay across it like she could physically prevent the room becoming somebody else's.

"What if one of us gets a different Order?" she asked.

Nobody pretended not to understand. Ava answered first.

"Then one of us gets a different Order."

"You have become annoyingly healthy about this."

"I had three weeks to panic first."

"Fair."

Jasper rested his head against the stone wall.

"Remember the first night?"

Harper said, "I saved someone from falling out the window." Ava corrected, "You pulled him back by the collar because he leaned too far."

"Saved."

"He was not falling."

"Preventative saving."

Jasper laughed. They remembered. Ava's labelled socks. Harper choosing the window bed before knowing anything. Jasper folding clothes exactly the way his mother taught him. The first supper. The sound of waves at night.

"I thought you were weird," Harper told Ava.

Ava stared.

"You introduced yourself like you were at a business meeting."

"I was nervous."

"We know that now."

Ava looked at Jasper.

"I thought you were quiet."

"I was quiet."

"You still are sometimes."

Harper pointed at him.

"I thought he looked like he was waiting for the building to speak."

Jasper froze.

"You did not."

"Something like that."

"You absolutely did not."

"Fine. I thought you looked scared."

That felt more believable.

"I was."

Harper rolled onto her back.

"I wasn't."

Ava said, "You told us you were maybe a little afraid of falling within ten minutes of arrival." Harper went quiet.

"Oh."

Jasper remembered. Of heights? No. Of falling? Maybe a little. Months later she had fallen. Water had moved. She had returned to the ledge. Now she could say she was scared without believing fear cancelled courage.

"That's annoying," Harper said.

"What?"

"Past me foreshadowed myself."

Jasper laughed so hard Ava told them both to be quiet. They failed. At some point the conversation became serious again. Ava sat cross-legged on the floor.

"We need one thing to agree now."

Harper groaned.

"Not another contingency."

"One."

"Fine."

Ava looked at both of them.

"If we are split tomorrow, nobody pretends they are fine because they think the other two are disappointed."

Jasper blinked. That was extremely specific. Which meant Ava had imagined doing exactly that. Harper understood too.

"Deal."

Jasper nodded.

"Deal."

Harper added, "And nobody says their Order is stupid because they wanted another one." Ava looked at her.

"That was your plan?"

"Possibly."

"Deal," Jasper said.

Ava nodded.

"Deal."

Jasper thought.

"And Saturday after breakfast. West Courtyard."

Their earlier promise. Harper held out her hand.

"Regardless."

Ava put hers on top. Jasper added his. Three hands. No magic. No oath spell. Nothing forcing them to keep it. That made it more meaningful. Harper looked down.

"This is very emotional."

Ava said, "You started it."

"I regret everything."

They released. Later, when the lantern dimmed automatically, Harper finally returned to her own mattress on the floor. The Bag Bed stood empty. Jasper climbed into bed. The western window was cracked open. Waves reached the room in steady sound. Water did not press against his awareness. Jasper was glad. Tomorrow the basin would be full of seawater. If Water chose to notice, Jasper would notice back. He would not ask. He would not test. He would let the academy ritual do what it had done for generations. And whatever platform waited, he would step onto it. Across the dark, Harper whispered.

"You awake?"

Ava answered, "Yes." Jasper said, "Yeah."

"Terrible."

"Go to sleep," Ava said.

"Can't."

"Try."

"How?"

"Close your eyes."

"Brilliant."

Jasper smiled into the dark. Eventually Harper stopped talking. Ava's breathing slowed. The Sea rolled against the cliff. Room Fourteen held them for one last ordinary night.
