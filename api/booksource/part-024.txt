"Why?"

CHAPTER SEVENTY-EIGHT
FAMILIES ON THE CLIFF

End-of-year family weekend made Tidefall feel too small. Parents filled corridors. Younger siblings stared at everything. Older siblings pretended not to. The dining hall somehow produced enough lunch for hundreds of visitors without anyone seeing the kitchens panic. Jasper's mother hugged him hard enough to lift one foot off the ground.

"Mum."

"You got taller."

"I did not."

His father inspected him critically.

"Maybe four millimetres."

"Traitor."

Jasper had spent midyear break at home, but the academy visit felt different. His parents were entering the version of Tidefall he had built inside his head. He wanted them to like Room Fourteen. Wanted them to meet Harper and Ava properly instead of as names in letters and mirror calls. That last part happened immediately. Harper's parents arrived while Jasper's mum was examining the Bag Bed.

"This is the empty one?"

"Storage," Jasper said.

Harper came through the door carrying three bags at once.

"Temporary national archive."

Her mother followed.

"Harper Vale, you were told to let your father carry one."

"I can carry three."

"That was not the instruction."

Jasper's mother looked at Jasper.

"Yes," he said. "She's always like this."

Harper stared.

"Betrayal in front of family."

Ava arrived with her parents ten minutes later. Her father shook Jasper's hand very seriously. Her mother looked at the labelled shelves and said, "Ava, I can tell which side is yours." Ava frowned.

"How?"

Everyone laughed. For the first hour, the parents talked mostly to their own children. Then something shifted. Jasper's mother asked Harper about the handball trophy. Harper's father asked Ava about the safety hooks. Ava's mother wanted to see Jasper's tiny lift model because Soren's design had appeared in one of his letters. The protected observation courtyard. Not the ledge where Harper fell. Nobody asked.

At the lower harbour viewing gallery, Jasper's mother stopped beside the glass. The repaired tide gate stood below. New reinforcement lines crossed the breakwater.

"That's where the storm was?"

"Yeah."

She looked at him. Not the gate.

"And you were in the shelter."

"Yeah. Mostly."

"Mostly?"

Jasper winced. His father looked interested in the ceiling.

"The bridge staff were still outside," Jasper explained. "I didn't go onto the bridge. I just... told the Sea they were there."

His mother was quiet.

"And it listened."

"It waited. I think."

"Because you asked?"

"Maybe. It chose."

That answer mattered to her.

"Good."

Jasper frowned.

"Good?"

"Good that you remember it wasn't your job to control a storm."

He leaned against the gallery rail.

"I know."

"Do you?"

"Mostly."

She smiled.

"That's more believable."

Harper's parents joined them. For a moment Jasper worried the conversation would turn toward Harper's fall. It did. But not how he expected. Harper's mother looked through the harbour glass.

"She told us the water moved."

Jasper glanced at Harper. Harper shrugged.

"Eventually."

Her father said, "She also told us you grabbed her the first time she slipped earlier in the year."

"That wasn't the big fall."

"We know."

He looked at Jasper.

"Thank you anyway."

Jasper felt awkward.

"I just... she was there."

"That's usually when helping happens."

Harper made a face.

"Can we stop making me a rescue documentary?"

Her mother touched her shoulder.

"Yes."

Simple. No argument. They moved on. At lunch, all three families sat together. This created chaos. Parents compared the versions of stories their children had told. The handball window incident gained new details. Ava discovered Harper had described her first-week sock labels in a letter home.

"Why would you tell your mother about my socks?"

"They were memorable."

Jasper discovered his mother had told his father about the jam homesickness moment after promising not to.

"I live there too," his father said.

"Privacy."

"Fair. Sorry."

That apology arrived without drama. Ava noticed. Jasper knew because Ava noticed apologies like weather instruments noticed pressure. In the afternoon, families attended an Order information session. This one was designed for adults. It focused on practical differences. Residence areas. Mentoring. Shared responsibilities. Order-specific traditions. What did not change. Classes remained mixed. Friends could visit other Order common spaces during open hours. Meals stayed shared. No Order had academic advantages. No Order was considered better. Jasper's mother raised her hand.

"If children are unhappy with placement?"

The staff member nodded.

Harper whispered, "So technically appealable." Ava whispered back, "Do not plan a legal case before being placed."

At sunset, families were allowed onto the upper west terrace. The Sea spread gold below. Jasper stood between his parents. Water was there. Quiet. He wondered whether it noticed them. She squeezed his shoulder.

"That sounds right."

When families left Sunday afternoon, Room Fourteen felt suddenly enormous. Harper sat on her bed.

"Your mum is scary."

Jasper stared.

"Your mum told you to put sunscreen on while you were already wearing sunscreen."

"Exactly. Scary recognizes scary."

Ava lay back on her pillow.

"My parents like both of you."

Harper gasped.

"Even me?"

"Against expectation."

Jasper laughed. The room settled around them. Three weeks had become ten days. Choosing Tide waited at the end. But now their families had seen the basin model. Seen the school. Seen them together. The future felt less like a door closing. More like several doors opening at once.

CHAPTER SEVENTY-NINE
WHAT THE SCHOOL REMEMBERS

Ava found her own sentence in the new safety handbook. Not exactly. The wording had changed. But she knew where it came from.

IF AN EVENT CANNOT YET BE EXPLAINED, RECORD OBSERVATIONS SEPARATELY FROM INTERPRETATION.

Ava read it three times. Then took the handbook to Crowe. Crowe was in the archive office surrounded by boxes. The room smelled like paper, dust and whatever tea she forgot to drink. Ava placed the handbook on her desk.

"This is mine."

Crowe read the line.

"The concept is not exclusively yours."

"I wrote nearly that exact sentence in my Deepwater notes."

Crowe looked up.

"Would you like attribution in the safety manual?"

Ava considered.

"No."

"Then what are we discussing?"

Ava sat.

"I don't know."

That was rare enough that Crowe put down her pen. Ava looked at the boxes.

"Did the school really forget Deepwater?"

"No."

"Then why were the records so bad?"

Crowe leaned back.

"Because forgetting is not the only way institutions lose knowledge."

Ava waited.

"Sometimes a person knows and retires. Sometimes a record is sealed for a good reason and nobody writes the safe version that could remain open. Sometimes people are embarrassed. Sometimes they believe a dangerous event is unique and not worth teaching broadly. Sometimes everyone assumes someone else remembers the important part."

Ava looked at the handbook.

"So the information exists but stops moving."

"Exactly."

"Like a blocked tide gate."

Crowe smiled slightly.

"Jasper has infected your metaphors."

"Unfortunately."

Crowe opened one of the boxes. Inside were copies of first-year incident reports from decades of school history. Minor burns. Falls. Broken equipment. Spell mistakes. Most boring. Boring was good.

Ava looked at her.

"Because of Deepwater."

"'Do not access restricted areas' is useless if the document doesn't define restricted."

Crowe changed it.

"'Seek staff assistance as appropriate' is vague."

Changed.

"This says 'avoid excessive magical exertion.' How much is excessive?"

Crowe sighed.

"That one requires medical guidance. Mark it."

Ava did. By dinner she had covered five pages in green annotations. Crowe looked exhausted.

"You asked."

"I did."

"Regret?"

"Some."

Ava smiled. That evening she told Harper and Jasper about the handbook. Harper was lying upside down on her bed.

"You edited school rules for fun."

"Not rules. Safety guidance."

"Worse."

Jasper picked up the draft. One new paragraph concerned unusual magical sensitivities. STUDENTS ARE NOT REQUIRED TO DEMONSTRATE, REPRODUCE OR USE UNUSUAL SENSORY OR MAGICAL ABILITIES FOR STAFF CURIOSITY, LESSONS OR RESEARCH. SAFETY-CRITICAL INFORMATION SHOULD BE REPORTED, BUT PARTICIPATION IN FURTHER TESTING REQUIRES CONSENT AND GUARDIAN INVOLVEMENT. His mother's fingerprints were all over the idea, even if not the wording. He read it twice.

"This is good."

Ava nodded.

"Crowe said it came from the post-storm review."

Harper flipped upright.

"So if some teacher says 'Jasper, make the ocean do the thing,' he can tell them to get stuffed."

Ava looked pained.

"Not the exact language."

"Spiritually."

Jasper smiled.

"I already could."

"Now there's paperwork supporting you."

Ava said this with deep satisfaction. Jasper laughed. The storm would not. Harper's fall would not. Benji's fall would not. Hooks. Consent rules. Clear histories. Updated harbour procedures. Maybe schools were too.

The safety handbook was not dramatic. Nobody would write legends about it. That was why it mattered. Deepwater had become a story because people failed to turn lessons into systems. The school remembered by changing what it did next. Jasper thought of the Chronicle line. You are allowed not to know yet. But once they learned something, they had to decide whether to carry it forward.

CHAPTER EIGHTY
THE HARBOUR AT LOW TIDE

Their final supervised lower-harbour visit happened at low tide. Jasper had seen the docks in rain. Storm. Morning fog. Bright winter sun. He had never seen them this exposed. Water pulled back from the lower wall, revealing black stone steps usually hidden beneath the surface. Barnacles crusted old posts. Green weed clung to the base of the repaired tide gate. The work barge lost during the storm had been recovered weeks earlier and now sat farther along the harbour with one fresh section of railing that did not match the rest. Professor Elowen led the group down.

"This is a maintenance lesson, not an invitation to lick barnacles."

Theo lowered his hand. Harper stared at him.

"Why was that your question?"

"It wasn't."

"Your hand was up."

"Separate matter."

The class split into groups. Jasper, Harper and Ava were assigned to inspect the new equipment bays and compare them with the old lower-dock storage system. Ava loved this. Harper survived it. Jasper kept looking at the gate. Not because Water called him. Because the storm had changed the place inside his memory. He remembered adults crossing the bridge. Ava counting surge spacing. Harper pulling frightened students away from shelter doors. The moment Jasper had shown the Sea an empty bridge and then waited. The surge arriving by its own choice. Now the gate moved smoothly. Ordinary water passed through. No emergency. A maintenance worker waved from the control platform. Jasper waved back.

"You all right?" Harper asked.

"Yeah."

She waited. He smiled.

"Actually yeah."

That seemed to satisfy her. At the far end of the lower dock stood the old Deepwater marker. Not the exact original. A replacement plate showed where earlier safety boundaries had once been. Students were allowed to view it from behind the modern line. Elowen explained without drama.

"The old lower boundary was here. After Deepwater, the supervised zone moved back. Later engineering work changed it again."

Benji asked, "Why keep the marker?"

"So future people know the older layout existed."

Ava nodded approvingly. Harper whispered, "You have a fan club." Ava ignored her. Jasper felt Water while Elowen spoke. A deep cool awareness. It came from beyond the harbour mouth. He looked outward. Low tide exposed more rock than usual. Small waves folded around the entrance. Water noticed the group. Or Jasper. Or the place. He could not tell. A faint image touched him. "Safety relevant?"

The phrase had become routine.

"No. Maybe memory."

"Do you want to record it?"

Jasper thought.

"Yes. But as uncertain."

SOURCE UNCERTAIN: COULD BE SEA RESPONSE, JASPER'S MEMORY OF ARCHIVE IMAGES, OR COMBINATION. That was the whole report. No one opened a sealed archive because of it. No one asked Jasper to recreate it. Later, the class helped wash salt from equipment and carry field boxes upstairs. Harper took the heaviest box. Ava took the inventory sheet. Jasper carried two smaller cases. At the top stair he turned once. The harbour looked tiny below. First year had pulled him toward that place again and again. Forbidden door. Old markers. Warnings. Storm. Year Two. Year Three.

Now he was leaving it after an authorized school lesson carrying a box of measuring ropes. That felt like a better ending for the docks than another mystery. Next year he would still be too young to go there alone. Then Year Four would change the rule. At seven, three years sounded enormous. Harper reached the courtyard first and put down her box.

"I'm coming back here the exact morning we're allowed without staff."

Ava said, "That is three years away."

"Calendar."

"You cannot calendar an event three years in advance."

Harper looked at her. Ava stopped.

"Actually, you can. Do not."

Harper grinned. Jasper knew she absolutely would.

CHAPTER EIGHTY-ONE
ONE SENTENCE

The second message from the former Deepwater student was shorter than the first. It was not addressed to Jasper. That mattered. Thorne asked him to come to the Headmaster's study after lunch. Harper and Ava did not come this time, not because they had been excluded from something dangerous, but because another person's privacy belonged partly to that person. "Very. They gave permission for me to share one sentence with you."

Jasper waited. Thorne read it aloud.

Jasper sat back. The sentence was not dramatic enough to sound like ancient wisdom. That made it harder to dismiss.

"Do they still sense it?"

"Rarely."

Jasper looked toward the western window.

"Do they miss it?"

"You can be careful without becoming afraid of yourself," Thorne said. "And you can listen without turning listening into the only thing you are."

Jasper nodded. When he left, he did not tell Harper and Ava the private parts of the conversation. He shared only the sentence Thorne had been allowed to share. Ava wrote it into the Chronicle. Harper underlined the word needing.
