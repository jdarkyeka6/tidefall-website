Ava immediately opened her notebook. Jasper looked out the window as the bus passed between two limestone ridges. For three seconds the sea disappeared behind rock. When it returned, the inlet beyond it was far wider than the coastline they had just entered should have allowed. No flash. No portal. Australia had simply made room. Deliveries came through controlled routes. Emergency services shared information with ordinary agencies when needed. Nothing about it felt like a secret kingdom. "Exactly."

Ava said, "Your purchasing logic is concerning." Jasper bought it. On the bus back, Lowtide disappeared more slowly than it had appeared. Buildings narrowed into the inlet, the inlet folded behind limestone, and eventually the road looked like an ordinary coastal road again. Jasper watched until the last roof vanished. For most of the year Tidefall had felt like the magical world. Lowtide corrected that. Tidefall was only one place inside it.

CHAPTER NINETY-ONE
THE LIBRARY CHALLENGE

The library's final challenge contained no secret books.

Ava was disappointed.

"Not disappointed," she corrected.

Harper looked at her. "Your face did disappointment."

"I expected complexity."

"Disappointment with extra steps."

Librarian Imani Grey placed six cards on the table.

"Find an answer to each question using the library. You may ask staff for help. You may use catalogues. You may not enter restricted shelves. You may not move reference books out of their section, and you may not invent an answer because the real one is inconvenient."

Harper sighed. "Oppressive."

The questions looked ordinary.

When was the current west dining hall built?

Which local bird recorded the longest migration through the Tidefall region?

What year did the Academy stop using the old cliff lift?

Find two historical accounts that disagree about the same school tradition.

Find a recipe printed in a student publication before Jasper's parents were born.

Then the final card:

FIND ONE QUESTION THE LIBRARY CANNOT ANSWER CONFIDENTLY.

Ava read it twice.

"That is not research."

Grey smiled. "It may be the most important research."

The trio divided the first five questions.

Jasper found the dining hall answer in a building-history index.

Harper found the old recipe after accidentally opening the correct student magazine while searching for something else.

Ava complained this rewarded chaos.

Harper called it instinct.

The cliff-lift question led Jasper to Soren, who knew the catalogue number without checking.

"Why?"

"I have interests."

"You have a problem."

Soren returned to his book.

The conflicting-history question was harder.

Two student chronicles described the same old Tidefall bell tradition differently. One claimed students rang it before departing for dangerous sea work. Another claimed it marked safe returns.

Harper stared at both.

"Which one is right?"

Grey, passing nearby, said, "Excellent question."

Ava immediately began searching for a third source.

They found none.

"Could both be partly right?" Jasper asked.

Ava hated the possibility.

Then came the final card.

A question the library could not answer confidently.

Harper suggested, "Why is cafeteria toast always either white or burnt?"

Grey refused this on the grounds that the kitchen probably knew.

Jasper thought about Titans.

Ava did too.

They did not use that.

Too easy.

Instead Ava asked:

WHO FIRST CALLED THE WEST STAIR THE WEST STAIR?

They searched building plans.

Old maps.

Student guides.

Maintenance books.

Nothing.

The name appeared everywhere by a certain year and nowhere before it.

No record showed who started it.

Ava stared at the empty result.

"So the answer is unknown."

"Correct," Grey said.

"That feels like failing."

"No. You have evidence for the limits of the archive."

Ava looked at Jasper.

He smiled.

"Unknown is allowed."

"Do not use my own lesson against me."

Harper said, "It is everybody's lesson now."

Grey stamped their challenge card complete.

The trio left with no prize except a tiny paper bookmark and the deeply irritating knowledge that good research sometimes ended with nothing.

Ava put the bookmark in the history box.

Underneath, she wrote:

THE LIBRARY DOES NOT KNOW EVERYTHING.

Harper added:

AVA SURVIVED THIS INFORMATION.

Ava crossed out Harper's line.

Then rewrote it more neatly.

Jasper laughed.

CHAPTER NINETY-TWO
HARPER AND AVA

Jasper spent Thursday morning with Soren in the library workshop.

Harper discovered this over breakfast.

"Again?"

Jasper looked up from his toast. "He asked yesterday."

"You have become unavailable."

Ava turned a page in her book. "That is called having plans."

"Without us."

"Healthy."

Harper stared at Ava for several seconds.

"Fine. What are we doing?"

Ava did not have an answer.

That was how the two of them ended up walking toward the art courtyard with no Jasper, no schedule and no particular reason for going there.

At first they talked about Jasper.

They discussed his latest shield lesson, whether he was still staring at every patch of water as though it might speak, and whether Soren had finally convinced him that old lift mechanisms were interesting.

"We are failing at separate friendship."

Harper laughed. "Is there a rubric?"

"No."

The student craft room had been opened for end-of-year use, with leftover supplies from the Night Market spread across several tables. There was thread, paint, scrap wood, beads, fabric and enough glue to create at least one preventable disaster.

Harper picked up embroidery thread.

"Bracelets."

Ava looked at her. "Can you make one?"

"No."

"Excellent."

Harper looked at the string. "You even over-control thread."

Ava's fingers stopped.

The question had existed between them for months.

"Yes."

"You didn't look scared."

"I was calculating."

Harper glanced at her. "Same thing?"

"For me, sometimes."

Ava pulled the next knot more gently.

"If I can work out what happened, I don't have to think about everything I don't know yet."

"Yes."

"I thought you were just calm."

"I thought you were calm too."

Harper laughed once. "I was absolutely not calm."

Jasper had worried. Ava had measured. Teachers had recorded. Other children had whispered. Somewhere in all of that, Harper had been the one who actually went over the edge.

Harper frowned. "For what?"

"I turned it into a pattern very quickly."

"You were trying to work out whether it would happen again."

Harper thought about it, then nodded. "Fine. Accepted."

A second later she pointed at Ava's wrist.

"Your knot is wrong."

Ava stared.

It was.

Harper's face lit up.

"I have waited my entire life for this."

"Seven years."

"My entire life."

Their bracelets were not good.

Harper's had one loop that sat too loose. Ava's was slightly crooked because she had corrected it so many times that one side had shortened. They wore them anyway.

When they found Jasper at lunch, he noticed immediately.

"Where's mine?"

Harper stared at him. "You abandoned us."

"You said separate friendship was healthy."

Ava narrowed her eyes. "You have been listening to us too much."

Jasper smiled.

Harper eventually made him a yellow bracelet after lunch. It was worse than either of theirs.

He wore it anyway.

Later that afternoon Ava received the result for her coastal-history project.

Sixty-eight percent.

"Is sixty-eight bad?"

CHAPTER NINETY-THREE
THE ETHICS GAME

Nocten called it an ethics game.

Harper called it arguing with cards.

Both descriptions were accurate.

The last cross-Order workshop before Choosing Tide took place in a hall with four signs placed at the corners:

YES

NO

MAYBE

NEED MORE INFORMATION

A mentor read a situation aloud.

Students moved to whichever answer they thought was best.

No magic.

No points.

No winning.

Harper found this suspicious.

The first card was easy.

A FRIEND ASKS YOU TO USE MAGIC TO DO THEIR HOMEWORK FOR THEM. SHOULD YOU?

Most students went to NO.

Harper stood at MAYBE.

Ava stared at her.

"What if they're sick?"

The mentor said, "Does helping have to mean doing the work for them?"

Harper thought.

Then moved to NEED MORE INFORMATION.

"Fine."

The second card changed the room.

YOU FIND PART OF A PRIVATE NOTE THAT MAKES YOU THINK A FRIEND MAY BE IN DANGER. SHOULD YOU READ THE REST?

Ava went very still.

Jasper noticed.

She chose NEED MORE INFORMATION.

"Why?" the mentor asked.

"If the first part gives enough information to get an adult, reading more might only invade privacy. If the danger is immediate and the missing information matters, it could be different."

The mentor nodded.

Ava did not look at Jasper.

She did not need to.

The third card hit him instead.

A SPELL COULD STOP YOUR FRIEND FROM DOING SOMETHING RISKY. THEY HAVE TOLD YOU NOT TO CONTROL THEIR MOVEMENT. SHOULD YOU CAST IT?

Harper slowly turned her head toward Jasper.

He chose NEED MORE INFORMATION.

"So did I," Harper said.

Jasper ignored her.

The mentor asked why.

"If they are about to get hit by something, yes," Jasper said. "If I just think they might do something wrong, no."

Harper nodded.

That was almost exactly the lesson from their shield argument.

Another card:

YOU CAN KEEP SOMEBODY SAFE BY HIDING INFORMATION FROM THEM. SHOULD YOU?

The room split.

Students argued.

Some said yes if the information would cause panic.

Others said people deserved to know risks affecting them.

Ava moved twice before settling on NEED MORE INFORMATION.

Harper remained at NO.

"What if telling them makes it worse?" Jasper asked.

Harper shrugged. "Then explain better."

Simple.

Not always complete.

Still useful.

The final card was different.

YOU HAVE POWER THAT OTHER PEOPLE DO NOT HAVE. WHAT DO YOU OWE THEM?

There were no corner labels for that one.

Students sat on the floor.

The room became quieter.

A Velmor student said honesty.

A Calyx student said care.

Someone from Nocten said restraint.

Mia said, "Not showing off."

Harper looked personally attacked.

Jasper thought about Titan-sense.

He had spent months worrying that hearing Water meant he was responsible for everything Water did.

That could not be right.

He raised his hand.

"I think you owe people good choices about what you do. Not everything that happens because you exist."

The mentor considered this.

"Can you explain?"

Jasper tried.

"If I can sense something dangerous and I don't tell anyone, that's my choice. If I tell adults and the sea still does something, I didn't choose the sea."

Ava looked at him.

Harper smiled slightly.

The mentor nodded. "Responsibility without pretending you control the world."

Jasper liked that version.

At the end, students were asked whether the game had changed any answers.

Harper raised her hand.

"I still think arguing with cards is the correct name."

Nocten refused to adopt it.

Harper considered this proof that Nocten feared innovation.

CHAPTER NINETY-FOUR
JASPER IS ANNOYED

Jasper woke up annoyed.

There had been no dream and no pressure from the Water Titan, which meant he could not even blame anything interesting. He was simply in a bad mood.

Harper noticed before breakfast.

"You look angry at cereal."

"I'm not."

Ava looked at his bowl. "You have been stirring it for ninety seconds."

"Can you both not?"

Silence followed.

Jasper regretted the words immediately, but not enough to apologise yet.

The morning made things worse.

Two first-years asked whether the Water Titan would affect Jasper's Choosing Tide. A third wanted to know whether he could already "feel Brannor."

"I don't know," Jasper said the first time.

"Still don't know," he said the second.

"No," he said to the third.

By lunch, he felt less like a person and more like a weather station everybody had decided to question.

Harper sat opposite him and made a deliberate attempt to discuss anything else.

"Mia says the handball court line is crooked."

"It is."

"Ava says it does not affect the game."

Ava looked up. "Not enough to matter."

"It could."

Jasper knew what they were doing and appreciated it.

Then Harper asked, "You okay?"

Something in him snapped.

"Can everyone stop asking me that?"

Harper went quiet.

Jasper closed his eyes.

"Sorry."

Ava did not ask whether he meant it.

Instead she said, "Do you want space?"

"I don't know."

"Do you want us to stop asking questions for a while?"

"Yes."

"Done."

That simple answer helped more than any conversation Jasper could have had.

He spent the next hour in a quiet study room by himself. Nobody asked about Water. Nobody watched his face for signs of mysterious communication. Nobody tried to turn his silence into evidence.

Eventually he worked out what was bothering him.

He was tired of being interpreted.

If he looked out a window, somebody wondered whether he sensed the Titan. If he became quiet, people assumed he was worried. If a water-related event was coming, other students wanted predictions.

Jasper had helped create some of that. For months he had talked about the mystery constantly because he wanted answers.

He was still allowed to get tired of it.

Before afternoon snack he found Harper and Ava.

"Sorry I snapped."

Harper shrugged. "You said stop. We stopped."

"I should have said it before yelling."

Ava nodded. "Yes."

Harper elbowed her.

"What? He did."

Jasper laughed.

"I'm tired of everyone asking me about Water."

"We can tell them to stop," Harper said.

Jasper shook his head. "I think I need to say it. You can help if they ignore me."

Harper smiled. "Third no?"

"Third no."

The next student who asked about Choosing Tide received a different answer.

"I don't want to talk about Water right now."

The boy blinked. "Oh. Sorry."

Then he stopped.

No argument. No explanation.

Jasper's mood improved almost immediately.

Ava did not write anything down.

He noticed that too.

At lunch the next day, the harbour alarm rang three short pulses.

Jasper's heart jumped before he remembered the final tone was lower than a real emergency alarm.

Drill.

Not storm.
