CHAPTER EIGHTY-SIX
SERVICE DAY

The first day without normal classes began with work.

Harper considered this false advertising.

"They said lessons were finished."

Professor Ward handed her a pair of gardening gloves. "They are."

"This feels educational."

"That is unfortunate."

Service Day happened every year before Tidefall closed for summer. Students helped with whatever needed doing: cleaning shared rooms, checking equipment, sorting storage, packing supplies and preparing gardens for weeks with fewer people around.

It was not punishment.

Everybody did it.

The first-years chose supervised jobs from a board.

Harper selected harbour equipment before Jasper had finished reading the list.

Ava chose archive sorting.

Jasper looked at both of them.

For a moment, all three had the same instinct.

Same job.

Same place.

Then Harper said, "I'm doing harbour."

Ava said, "Archive."

Jasper smiled. "I'll do kitchens."

Harper stared at him. "Who are you people?"

They separated.

That, Jasper realised, was probably part of the point.

The kitchen job involved Chef Marlow, six first-years and enough shelves to make Jasper understand how Tidefall fed hundreds of people without anybody noticing the work behind it.

They checked dates, moved dry goods, counted sealed jars and separated summer storage from supplies that would remain ready for emergencies.

"Why do we keep so much food?" one child asked.

Chef Marlow looked toward the sea-facing wall.

"Because sometimes boats cannot reach us."

Simple.

No magic required.

During the storm, blankets, water and food had seemed to appear from nowhere.

They had not.

People had planned.

"Safety is boring before it matters," Chef Marlow said as Jasper labelled a shelf.

Jasper smiled. "Ava would like that."

"Ava likes anything in a labelled container."

"You know Ava?"

Chef Marlow gave him a look. "Everybody knows Ava."

Fair.

At morning break Jasper found Harper in the lower courtyard wearing a faded work vest much too large for her.

There was black grease on one cheek.

She looked delighted.

"Harbour?"

"Best job."

"What did you do?"

"Tested life rings. Checked ropes. Lowered the little cargo winch."

"Magic?"

"Hand crank."

Harper said this as though the hand crank had personally defeated magic.

Jasper held up the roll of pantry labels in his pocket.

"I counted lentils."

Harper looked genuinely sorry for him.

"Tragic."

Ava joined them carrying three archive folders.

"I found six records filed under the wrong year."

Harper looked at Jasper. "She's having the best day of her life."

Ava ignored her. "Also, an old maintenance ledger mentions a western cistern valve that is not on the modern map."

Harper's eyes lit up.

Ava immediately added, "I gave it to Professor Crowe."

The light went out again.

"We are not investigating?"

"No."

"What if it is secret?"

"It is probably a valve."

"That is what a secret valve would want you to think."

Jasper laughed.

After break, the three returned to their separate jobs.

Jasper packed sealed surplus food into donation boxes for a coastal community pantry. Harper helped clean and test harbour safety equipment. Ava finished the archive sort without opening a single file she was not authorised to read, which she privately considered a greater achievement than anyone else understood.

They met again near dinner.

Harper smelled like rope and machine oil.

Jasper smelled like flour.

Ava had a pencil behind one ear.

"Good day?" Jasper asked.

Harper said, "Obviously."

Ava nodded.

Jasper thought about how strange that would have sounded in first term.

Back then, being separated had felt like something had gone wrong.

Now they could spend a whole morning apart and return with different stories.

Nothing had been lost.

They were still Room Fourteen.

Just not only Room Fourteen.

CHAPTER EIGHTY-SEVEN
MUSIC UNDER THE ARCHES

Tidefall's end-of-year music evening had one rule.

Nobody had to perform.

Harper performed.

This surprised Jasper, Ava and, judging by her expression when she first agreed to it, Harper herself.

The music department opened Founders' Courtyard after dinner for an informal concert. Older students brought instruments. Younger students were allowed to perform in groups if a teacher had approved what they were doing. Everybody else sat on blankets, ate snacks and tried not to ruin the quiet songs by opening loud food wrappers.

Jasper intended to specialise in sitting.

Ava did too.

Harper disappeared after lunch.

At six thirty she walked onto the little stage carrying a hand drum.

Mia followed with another.

Theo carried a third and looked far too pleased with himself.

Jasper stared. "What is happening?"

Ava looked equally surprised. "Apparently planning."

Harper saw them and grinned.

Then the three children onstage began.

The rhythm was simple.

It also was not perfect.

Harper came in early once. Theo nearly dropped a drumstick. Mia laughed halfway through and had to catch the beat again.

Nobody stopped.

They found one another.

The rhythm grew steadier until older students started clapping along beneath the arches.

Jasper realised why Harper had not told them.

If she had announced the performance three days earlier, she would have spent three days trying to make it a competition.

Instead she had learned one rhythm.

With other people.

Enough.

The rest of the evening moved between polished older-student performances and younger children doing things they had learned because they liked them.

Tidefall's magic did not make the evening less familiar.

That was what Jasper liked most.

Music under stone arches still sounded like music.

Harper did not rush back to the stage.

She sat with Jasper and Ava and tapped the beat against her knees.

Jasper looked at the three bracelets on their wrists.

Pink.

Green.

Yellow.

Bad knots.

Good enough.

The evening did not need one.

On the walk back to West Residence, Harper said, "I was good."

Ava considered. "You were adequate."

Harper looked wounded.

Then Ava smiled.

"Also good."

Harper bumped her shoulder.

Jasper walked between them.

For once, the sound following them through Tidefall had nothing to do with the sea.

CHAPTER EIGHTY-EIGHT
AVA WITHOUT A PLAN

Ava gave Professor Elowen her notebook for one afternoon.

She did not lose it.

She did not forget it.

She handed it over on purpose.

Harper had suggested the experiment, which was the first reason Ava distrusted it.

"You said you wanted to get better at not planning everything," Harper reminded her.

"I said I wanted to tolerate uncertainty."

"Same thing with extra syllables."

"It is not."

Jasper sat between them on the west steps. "What is the actual plan?"

Ava glared at him.

"Do not encourage that word."

The exercise was simple.

One free afternoon.

No notebook.

No written schedule.

No activity chosen in advance.

Meals and curfew remained fixed because Professor Elowen refused to let personal growth interfere with dinner.

Ava held the notebook out.

Elowen took it.

Ava did not release it.

Elowen waited.

Ava finally let go.

Harper whispered, "Brave."

"I can still hurt you without notes."

"Excellent."

They left the office.

Immediately Harper asked, "What now?"

Ava stopped walking.

Jasper said nothing.

This was hers.

Library to the left.

Courtyard to the right.

Stairs ahead.

Ava stared at all three choices as though Tidefall had designed them specifically to be annoying.

"Walk," she decided.

"Where?" Harper asked.

"Forward."

They walked forward.

That led to the kitchens.

A tray of biscuits was cooling near the service counter.

Harper called this proof that uncertainty was good.

Ava called it coincidence.

They were allowed one biscuit each.

Afterward, they reached the eastern courtyard.

A group of students were painting small wooden signs for the summer gardens.

Ava nearly said they had no time.

Then remembered they had nowhere to be.

They stayed.

Her first sign was perfectly centred.

Harper's was crooked.

Jasper's had paint on the back because he put it down in the wrong place.

Ava started to correct Harper's lettering.

Then stopped.

"It's wrong," Harper said.

"Yes."

"And you are leaving it."

"Yes."

Harper looked frightened.

They carried the finished signs to the garden under supervision.

On the way back, rain began without appearing on Ava's weather notes because Ava did not have weather notes.

She stood beneath an arch watching the courtyard darken.

"I would have packed umbrellas."

Harper said, "We are under a roof."

"That is not the point."

Jasper looked at the rain. "What's the worst thing happening?"

"We wait."

They waited.

Ten minutes later the rain softened.

Nothing had gone wrong.

Ava disliked how obvious the lesson was.

Later they found Soren repairing a mechanical model in the library workshop and stayed to watch. Then they wandered through the kitchens again because Harper believed uncertainty might produce another biscuit.

It did not.

By late afternoon, Ava had stopped checking the time every five minutes.

She still checked.

Just less.

When they returned to Elowen's office, the notebook sat exactly where Ava had left it.

Elowen handed it over.

Ava opened to a blank page.

Her pencil hovered.

Harper leaned over.

"Are you going to record everything?"

Ava thought about the biscuits, the paint, the rain and the fact that none of it required measurement to have happened.

She wrote one line.

NOTHING BAD HAPPENED.

Then another.

ALSO BISCUIT.

Harper approved.

Ava closed the notebook.

She had not learned to stop planning.

She did not want to.

Planning was useful.

She had learned something smaller.

A plan could be missing without the world becoming dangerous.

For Ava, that was enormous.

CHAPTER EIGHTY-NINE
OPEN DOORS

On the final Wednesday of term, first-year teachers left their classroom doors open.

Not literally every door.

Professor Ashcroft argued that leaving his open ruined the temperature control.

The tradition was called Open Doors anyway.

Students could visit teachers without an assignment, return borrowed things, look at next year's materials or ask questions they had never managed to ask during class.

Harper disliked the emotional premise.

"We see them next year."

Ava said, "Not necessarily for the same classes."

"Still alive in the building."

"Presumably."

Jasper visited Professor Ward first.

The Foundations room looked wrong without rows of students.

Practice objects had been boxed.

Feather jars were empty.

Thorne looked alarmed.

The three left before he could recover.

Open Doors ended without speeches.

Teachers packed rooms.

Students packed trunks.

Tidefall kept moving toward summer.

Goodbyes, Jasper was discovering, did not always need to sound final.

CHAPTER NINETY
LOWTIDE TOWN

The first-years spent almost an entire year at Tidefall before the Academy took them into Lowtide Town. Harper considered this evidence that the adults had been hiding something excellent. Professor Elowen considered it evidence that seven-year-olds required several months of basic safety training before being taken anywhere interesting.

Lowtide lay several kilometres south of the Academy, although maps were not especially helpful once the road entered the concealed coastal zone. The town was still physically in Australia. The ocean beside it was the same ocean Jasper could see from ordinary beaches near Perth, and a determined person with the correct route could travel between Lowtide and the outside world without crossing into another realm. The strange part was the space in between.

Tidefall's old concealment magic worked in two layers. The first was perceptual: roads looked less inviting than they were, distant buildings seemed unimportant, and people without permission tended to remember perfectly reasonable reasons to turn around. The second was geographical magic so old that even teachers disagreed about its neatest explanation.
